  1. 1. (1 pt) Questions 1–3 refer to the differential equation below for a damped oscillator of mass mm, where γ\gamma and ω\omega are positive constants. Its three left-hand-side terms are labeled A, B and C: md2dt2x(t)⏟𝖠+2mγddtx(t)⏟𝖡+mω2x(t)⏟𝖢=0\underbrace{m\,\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\,x(t)}_{\textsf{A}} + \underbrace{2m\gamma\,\frac{d}{dt}\,x(t)}_{\textsf{B}} + \underbrace{m\,\omega^{2}\,x(t)}_{\textsf{C}} = 0

    Which term represents the restoring force?

    1. term A
    2. term B
    3. term C
    4. 00 (the right-hand side)
    Solution for question 1

    Correct answer: C. term C

    Why: Term C, mω2xm\omega^{2}x, is proportional to the displacement xx. With ω2=k/m\omega^{2} = k/m it is kxkx: Hooke’s law. Newton’s second law reads md2xdt2=−2mγdxdt−mω2x,m\,\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}} = -2m\gamma\,\frac{dx}{dt} - m\omega^{2}x, and the force −mω2x-m\omega^{2}x always points back toward x=0x = 0. Moving it to the left-hand side gives term C. For a pendulum it is m(g/L)xm(g/L)\,x.

    Watch out: Choice A, md2xdt2m\,\tfrac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}}, is mass times acceleration — the “mama” side of Newton’s second law, not a force that anything exerts on the oscillator. Choice B is proportional to the velocity dx/dtdx/dt, which makes it the damping force (Question 2); it vanishes when γ=0\gamma = 0. Choice D: the right-hand side is 00 because nothing drives the oscillator from outside — it is not one of the forces from the spring or the drag.

  2. 2. (1 pt) Which term represents the damping force?

    1. term A
    2. term B
    3. term C
    4. 00 (the right-hand side)
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: B. term B

    Why: Term B, 2mγdxdt2m\gamma\,\tfrac{dx}{dt}, is proportional to the velocity, like viscous drag Fdrag=−bdxdtF_{\text{drag}} = -b\,\tfrac{dx}{dt} with b=2mγb = 2m\gamma (moved to the left-hand side). Set γ=0\gamma = 0 and it disappears, leaving the undamped oscillator of Quiz 5.

    Watch out: Choice A is mass times acceleration (inertia), the “mama” in F=maF = ma; it is there with or without damping. Choice C is proportional to the displacement: the restoring (spring) force of Question 1, which does not depend on γ\gamma. Choice D: the right-hand side would hold an external driving force, and there is none. Damping is a force from the surroundings that depends on the motion itself, so it sits on the left with xx.

  3. 3. (3 pts) Sketch a graph of a valid solution x(t)x(t) of the differential equation above, for a weakly damped oscillator, γ≪ω\gamma \ll \omega, but sketch it so that it is obvious the damping is not zero. Put the displacement xx on the vertical axis and time tt on the horizontal axis, label both axes, and show several cycles.

    Solution for question 3

    The solution. For γ≪ω\gamma \ll \omega (Question 4), x(t)=Ae−γtcos⁡(ωdt),ωd=ω2−γ2≈ω,x(t) = A\,e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_{d}t), \qquad \omega_{d} = \sqrt{\omega^{2} - \gamma^{2}} \approx \omega, a cosine (or sine) whose amplitude shrinks inside the envelope ±Ae−γt\pm A\,e^{-\gamma t}.

    Oscillation. Smooth, centered on x=0x = 0 (the tt-axis), with an essentially constant period T≈2π/ωT \approx 2\pi/\omega, crossing zero between every crest and trough.

    Damping visibly not zero. Each crest is lower than the one before, by the same factor each cycle (e−γTe^{-\gamma T}), so the crests trace a decaying exponential: steep at first, then flatter, approaching 00 without ever stopping abruptly.

    Weak, not strong. Because γ≪ω\gamma \ll \omega, the decay is slow on the scale of one period: several full cycles before the motion becomes small. A curve that dies out within a cycle, or never swings through zero at all, is strong damping.

    Axes. Displacement xx on the vertical axis, time tt on the horizontal axis — not the other way round.

    Any start is fine. No initial condition is given, so starting at a crest (cosine), at zero (sine), or anywhere else on the decaying oscillation is acceptable.

    Two slips cost points: an oscillation about a level above the tt-axis (the oscillator swings about its equilibrium, x=0x = 0), and missing or swapped axis labels.

    1. (1 pt) If time tt is measured in seconds, what is the physical unit of −γ+γ2−ω2-\gamma + \sqrt{\gamma^{2} - \omega^{2}}? (Fill in one bubble.)

      2. inverse seconds
      3. seconds squared
      4. radians

      Solution — part (a) of question 4

      Inverse seconds — B. Divide the equation by mm: d2xdt2+2γdxdt+ω2x=0\tfrac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}} + 2\gamma\,\tfrac{dx}{dt} + \omega^{2}x = 0. Every term has the unit of an acceleration, m/s2\text{m/s}^{2}. Since dxdt\tfrac{dx}{dt} is in m/s, γ\gamma is in s−1\text{s}^{-1}; since xx is in m, ω2\omega^{2} is in s−2\text{s}^{-2}. So γ2−ω2\gamma^{2} - \omega^{2} is in s−2\text{s}^{-2}, its square root in s−1\text{s}^{-1}, and so is the sum. Check: XtXt in eXte^{Xt} is then a pure number, as an exponent must be.

      Choice E, unitless, is the unit of the whole exponent XtXt — not of XX, which must cancel the seconds of tt. It is the same distinction as γ\gamma versus γt\gamma t on the midterm (Questions 1–2). Choice A is upside down (seconds is the unit of 1/γ1/\gamma, the decay time), and choice C is upside down and squared. Choice D: ω\omega is in radians per second; a radian is a pure number, so rad/s\text{rad/s} is s−1\text{s}^{-1}.

      Ae−γtA\,e^{-\gamma t}

      eγ2−ω2te^{\sqrt{\gamma^{2} - \omega^{2}}\,t}

Where the class had the most trouble

These cost the most points. If you lost points here, start your review with them.

  1. Question 4(b) — explaining the two factors. Most of the class got the algebra going: 8 of 12 brought ii out of the square root. But only 4 said that eiωte^{i\omega t} oscillates, and only 2 said that e−γte^{-\gamma t} shrinks the amplitude — and those two sentences are the explanation the question asks for. Several answers stopped at Ae−γteiωdtA\,e^{-\gamma t}\,e^{i\omega_{d}t} with no interpretation, dropped γ\gamma (an AeiωtA\,e^{i\omega t} that never decays is not damped), or substituted back into the differential equation, which checks a solution but does not explain it. Only 5 of 12 split eXte^{Xt} into the two factors.
  2. Extra credit — reading the period off the screen. Only one booklet placed the peak at 200 Hz. Count divisions per period (2.5), multiply by 2 ms, then invert: f=1/(5ms)=200f = 1/(5\ \text{ms}) = 200 Hz. A peak at 0.2 on an axis labeled Hz is at 0.2 Hz — keep the unit, 0.2ms−1=200Hz0.2\ \text{ms}^{-1} = 200\ \text{Hz}. Other slips: a period misread as 1 ms (a peak at 1000 Hz), plotting the period itself, drawing harmonics for a pure sine, or sketching the signal in time instead of its transform.
  3. Question 4(a) — XX versus XtXt. Both wrong answers were “unitless”, the unit of the whole exponent XtXt. XX itself is in inverse seconds.
  4. Question 3 — axes and centering. Every sketch showed a decaying oscillation over three or more cycles. The points lost were axis labels (missing, or xx and tt swapped) and oscillations about a level above the tt-axis instead of about x=0x = 0.
