  3. 3. (1.5 pts) Which of these can a Fourier transform spectrum |X(f)||X(f)|, like the ones we plotted in class and in the homework, tell you? Choose all that apply. (0.3 points for each choice marked correctly: filled if it is correct, left empty if it is not.)

    • A. The frequencies (harmonics) present in the waveform
    • B. The relative amplitudes of these harmonics
    • C. The relative phase differences among the harmonics
    • D. The strength of damping or decay in each harmonic (from the spectrum’s peak width)
    • E. Your instructor’s annual salary
    Solution for question 3

    A, B and D.

    • A — yes. Each harmonic shows up as a peak at its own frequency: the sung note’s fundamental and overtones, the cantilever’s 72 kHz and 448 kHz modes, the 500 Hz and 3000 Hz of Question 4.
    • B — yes. The height of each peak follows that harmonic’s amplitude (Day 3 lecture: a harmonic with 0.35 of the amplitude gives a peak 35% as tall).
    • C — no. Shifting one harmonic’s phase changes the shape of the waveform but leaves the magnitude spectrum |X(f)||X(f)| exactly the same (Day 3 lecture, section 5). The phases came from curve fitting in the bimodal-AFM homework, 3(c).
    • D — yes. A harmonic that decays at rate γ\gamma lasts only about 1/γ1/\gamma, and its peak is correspondingly broad, of width about γ\gamma (the NMR linewidth). Faster decay, broader peak.
    • E — no. Sadly, no peak at that frequency.

  6. EC. (1.5 pts) Extra credit. (a) Write the formula (definition, with the integral) for the Fourier transform X(f)X(f) of a waveform x(t)x(t). (b) In the theoretical definition, what are the bounds (limits) of the integral? (c) In reality, when we analyze real data, do we ever take the integral over those bounds? Explain briefly.

    Solution for question EC

    (a) Multiply the waveform by a reference sine and add up the area, as on Wednesday’s “Fourier by Hand” worksheet: X(f)=∫−∞+∞x(t)sin⁡(2πft)dtor, in full,X(f)=∫−∞+∞x(t)e−i2πftdt.X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x(t)\,\sin(2\pi f t)\,dt \qquad \text{or, in full,} \qquad X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x(t)\,e^{-i 2\pi f t}\,dt. The reference sine (or e−i2πfte^{-i2\pi ft}) is essential: ∫x(t)dt\int x(t)\,dt alone is just the area under the waveform, the same number for every ff.

    (b) From t=−∞t = -\infty to t=+∞t = +\infty: all time.

    (c) No. Real data exist only for the finite time we record, so the integral (in practice a sum over the samples, the FFT) runs only over the record, from 00 to TT (20 ms on the worksheet, 500 μs in the bimodal-AFM homework). That is why a real peak is never infinitely tall and is about 1/T1/T wide: record longer for a taller, narrower peak.

Where the class had the most trouble

These cost the most points. If you lost points here, start your review with them.

  1. Question 4 — the fast ripple. Half the class (6 of 12) wrote a fast period between 0.2 and 0.5 ms, and a seventh counted the ripples to the right frequency but wrote the wrong period. The misses (0.6 to 2 ms) came from counting only some of the ripples, or none. There are six ripples per slow cycle: the bumps on the crests and troughs, and the shoulders on the steep slopes. Mark them on the graph, count them over a whole slow cycle (or over several), and divide.
  2. Question 4 — units on the answers. Three booklets wrote the periods as bare numbers (“2”, “0.75”) with no time unit, and one wrote 1/(2ms)1/(2\ \text{ms}) as 0.5×10−30.5 \times 10^{-3} Hz. One over milliseconds is kilohertz: 1/(2ms)=0.5kHz=5001/(2\ \text{ms}) = 0.5\ \text{kHz} = 500 Hz. Also check that each period and its frequency agree: f=1/Tf = 1/T.
  3. Extra credit (a) — what goes inside the integral. Only 3 of 12 wrote x(t)x(t) times a sine (or e−i2πfte^{-i2\pi ft}). Without the reference sine, ∫x(t)dt\int x(t)\,dt is just the area under the waveform and cannot pick out a frequency. Most of the class did get the bounds (−∞-\infty to ∞\infty) and why real data use a finite record.
  4. Question 1 — bringing out ii, and keeping e−γte^{-\gamma t}. Seven of 12 got the ii step fully right. The slips: a sign flipped under the root (γ2+ω2\gamma^{2} + \omega^{2}, which never gives ii), ii written outside a root whose inside was left unchanged, and setting γ=0\gamma = 0, which removes the decay the plot shows. Substituting into the differential equation checks a solution but does not explain the plot.
  5. Question 5 — labels and heights. Most sketches were right. The points lost: an axis with numbers but no name or unit, the wrong prefix (MHz), the tall peak drawn at the higher frequency or equal heights, and a spectrum drawn on a time axis.
