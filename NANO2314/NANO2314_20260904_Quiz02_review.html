  1. 1. (1 pt) What is the motor's acceleration at t=6t = 6 s?

    1. −6μm/s2-6\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    2. −2μm/s2-2\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    3. 0μm/s20\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    4. 2μm/s22\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    5. 6μm/s26\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    Solution for question 1

    Correct answer: C. a=0μm/s2a = 0\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}

    Why: Acceleration is the slope of the v(t)v(t) graph, not its height. At t=6t = 6 s the motor is in the constant-speed phase (33 s to 77 s), where the graph is flat. A flat line has zero slope, so a=0a = 0. The motor is moving fast, but it is not changing speed.

    Watch out: choice E (6μm/s26\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}) is the number you get by reading the height of the curve off the vertical axis — but that is the speed, not the acceleration. Choice D (2μm/s22\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}) is the slope of the ramp up, and choice B is the slope of the ramp down; both are the right calculation performed at the wrong time.

  2. 2. (1 pt) What is the motor's acceleration at t=8t = 8 s?

    1. −6μm/s2-6\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    2. −2μm/s2-2\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    3. 0μm/s20\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    4. 2μm/s22\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    5. 6μm/s26\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: B. a=−2μm/s2a = -2\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}

    Why: t=8t = 8 s lies on the ramp down, which runs from t=7t = 7 s to t=10t = 10 s. Take rise over run across that straight segment:

    a=ΔvΔt=0−610−7=−63=−2μm/s2.a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{0 - 6}{10 - 7} = \frac{-6}{3} = -2\ \mu\text{m/s}^{2}.

    The sign is negative because the motor is slowing down. Since the segment is straight, its slope is the same everywhere on it — you get −2-2 at t=8t = 8 s, at t=9t = 9 s, or anywhere else in that window.

    Watch out: choice A (−6-6) is Δv\Delta v by itself — the change in speed, without dividing by the 33 s it took. An acceleration is always a rate, so that division is not optional.

  3. 3. (1 pt) How far does the motor travel from t=0t = 0 to t=10t = 10 s?

    1. 18μm18\ \mu\text{m}
    2. 24μm24\ \mu\text{m}
    3. 30μm30\ \mu\text{m}
    4. 42μm42\ \mu\text{m}
    5. 60μm60\ \mu\text{m}
    Solution for question 3

    Correct answer: D. 42μm42\ \mu\text{m}

    Why: Distance is the area under the v(t)v(t) curve — that is what ∫vdt\int v\,dt means. No calculus is needed here, because the region breaks cleanly into two triangles and a rectangle:

    12(3)(6)⏟9+(4)(6)⏟24+12(3)(6)⏟9=42μm.\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(3)(6)}_{9} \;+\; \underbrace{(4)(6)}_{24} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(3)(6)}_{9} \;=\; 42\ \mu\text{m}.

    Check the units as you go: μm/s\mu\text{m/s} times s gives μm\mu\text{m}, a distance. That check is the fastest way to confirm you multiplied a speed by a time and not something else.

    Watch out: choice E (6060) treats the whole 1010 s as one rectangle at top speed (6×106 \times 10), ignoring the ramps. Choice C (3030) treats the region as one big triangle (12(10)(6)\tfrac{1}{2}(10)(6)). Choice B (2424) is the rectangle alone, and choice A (1818) is the two triangles alone — each is a piece of the answer rather than the whole of it.

  4. 4. (1 pt) What is the motor's average speed over 0≤t≤100 \le t \le 10 s?

    1. 0.42μm/s0.42\ \mu\text{m/s}
    2. 3μm/s3\ \mu\text{m/s}
    3. 4.2μm/s4.2\ \mu\text{m/s}
    4. 6μm/s6\ \mu\text{m/s}
    5. 42μm/s42\ \mu\text{m/s}
    Solution for question 4

    Correct answer: C. 4.2μm/s4.2\ \mu\text{m/s}

    Why: Average speed is total distance divided by total time, and Question 3 already produced the distance:

    v‾=total distancetotal time=42μm10s=4.2μm/s.\bar{v} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}} = \frac{42\ \mu\text{m}}{10\ \text{s}} = 4.2\ \mu\text{m/s}.

    Geometrically, v‾\bar{v} is the height of the rectangle that has the same area as the region under v(t)v(t) — flatten the trapezoid out and this is the level it settles at.

    Watch out: choice B (33) is the tempting "average of the endpoints," (0+6)/2(0+6)/2. That would be correct only if the speed rose steadily across the whole interval. It does not: the motor sits at full speed for 44 of the 1010 seconds, which pulls the average above 33. Choice D (66) is the peak speed, choice E (4242) is the distance wearing the wrong unit, and choice A (0.420.42) is a decimal-point slip.

  5. 5. (1 pt) A different nanoscale motor's position is given by

    x(t)=αt2=αt−2,x(t) = \frac{\alpha}{t^{2}} = \alpha\, t^{-2},

    where xx is measured in μm\mu\text{m} and tt in seconds. What units must the constant α\alpha carry?

    1. μm/s2\mu\text{m/s}^{2}
    2. μm/s\mu\text{m/s}
    3. μm\mu\text{m}
    4. μm⋅s2\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{2}
    5. s2/μm\text{s}^{2}/\mu\text{m}
    Solution for question 5

    Correct answer: D. μm⋅s2\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{2}

    Why: Do not try to read the units off the formula by inspection — solve for the constant first, then read off what is left. Multiply both sides by t2t^{2}:

    x=αt2⇒α=xt2.x = \frac{\alpha}{t^{2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = x\,t^{2}.

    Now the units are forced: α\alpha is a length times a time squared, so [α]=μm⋅s2[\alpha] = \mu\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{2}.

    Then check it by substituting the units back into the original expression and confirming you land on a length:

    [α][t]2=μm⋅s2s2=μm.✓\frac{[\alpha]}{[t]^{2}} = \frac{\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{2}}{\text{s}^{2}} = \mu\text{m}. \;\checkmark

    Watch out: choice A (μm/s2\mu\text{m/s}^{2}) is the classic slip when an exponent is negative. Seeing t−2t^{-2} sitting next to α\alpha, it is easy to carry a "per second squared" into α\alpha as well — but the t−2t^{-2} already supplies that, so α\alpha has to cancel it, which means multiplying by s2\text{s}^{2}, not dividing. Whenever an exponent is negative, isolate the constant before you assign it units.

  6. 6. (1 pt) In general, what is the purpose of a for loop in Python?

    1. To define a new function that can be called later
    2. To repeat a block of code once for each item in a list, with the loop variable taking each item's value in turn
    3. To store rows and columns of data in a table
    4. To read data from a file on disk into memory
    5. To print output to the screen
    Solution for question 6

    Correct answer: B. To repeat a block of code once for each item in a list, with the loop variable taking each item's value in turn.

    Why: A for loop is Python's tool for repetition over a collection. The loop variable is rebound to the next item on every pass, and the indented block runs again with that new value:

    for t_idx in range(1, len(df)):
    dt = t[t_idx] - t[t_idx - 1]
    distance = distance + v[t_idx] * dt

    Here t_idx takes the values 1,2,3,…1, 2, 3, \dots in turn, and the two indented lines run once per value. That is exactly the numerical-integration pattern from the homework: add up one thin slice per time step — the same "area under the curve" idea as Question 3, done by computer.

    Watch out: every other choice names a real Python capability, just not this one. (A) is def, (C) is a DataFrame or an array, (D) is something like pd.read_csv, and (E) is print. None of them involve repeating anything — that is the word to anchor on.

  7. 7. (4 pts) A nanoscale motor's acceleration is

    a(t)=6t2−7t3,a(t) = 6t^{2} - 7t^{3},

    where aa is in nm/s2\text{nm/s}^{2} and tt is in s. Answer all parts below. Show your work — partial credit will be awarded.

    1. (2 pts) Find the indefinite integral ∫a(t)dt\int a(t)\, dt. Do not forget the constant of integration.

      Solution — part (a) of question 7

      Apply the reverse power rule ∫tndt=tn+1n+1\int t^{n}\,dt = \dfrac{t^{n+1}}{n+1} term by term — raise the exponent by one, then divide by the new exponent:

      ∫a(t)dt=∫(6t2−7t3)dt=6⋅t33−7⋅t44+C=2t3−74t4+C.\int a(t)\,dt = \int \left(6t^{2} - 7t^{3}\right) dt = 6\cdot\frac{t^{3}}{3} - 7\cdot\frac{t^{4}}{4} + C = 2t^{3} - \frac{7}{4}t^{4} + C.

      The +C+C is not decoration. Every function of the form 2t3−74t4+C2t^{3} - \tfrac{7}{4}t^{4} + C has exactly the same derivative, so without CC you have named only one member of an infinite family.

      What it means physically: integrating an acceleration gives a velocity, so this expression is the motor's v(t)v(t), in nm/s\text{nm/s}. The constant CC is the initial velocity v(0)v(0) — the one thing the acceleration alone can never tell you.

    2. (1 pt) How do you know you computed the integral correctly? Show a check of your answer from part (a).

      Solution — part (b) of question 7

      Differentiation undoes integration, so differentiate your own answer from part (a) and see whether the original a(t)a(t) comes back:

      ddt(2t3−74t4+C)=3⋅2t2−4⋅74t3+0=6t2−7t3=a(t).✓\frac{d}{dt}\left(2t^{3} - \frac{7}{4}t^{4} + C\right) = 3\cdot 2t^{2} - 4\cdot\frac{7}{4}t^{3} + 0 = 6t^{2} - 7t^{3} = a(t). \;\checkmark

      Term by term: 2t32t^{3} differentiates to 6t26t^{2}, and 74t4\tfrac{7}{4}t^{4} differentiates to 7t37t^{3}. Both match, and CC differentiates to zero whatever its value. This is a check you can run on any integral, on any exam, without being told the answer — which is precisely why the question asks for it.

    3. (1 pt) Evaluate the definite integral ∫01a(t)dt\int_{0}^{1} a(t)\, dt. Include the correct unit in your answer.

      Solution — part (c) of question 7

      By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, evaluate the antiderivative from part (a) at the upper limit and subtract its value at the lower limit. The +C+C cancels in that subtraction, so it can be dropped here:

      ∫01a(t)dt=[2t3−74t4]01=(2−74)−(0−0)=14=0.25nm/s.\int_{0}^{1} a(t)\,dt = \left[\, 2t^{3} - \frac{7}{4}t^{4} \,\right]_{0}^{1} = \left(2 - \frac{7}{4}\right) - (0 - 0) = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25\ \text{nm/s}.

      The unit is where this part is won or lost. Integrating an acceleration (nm/s2\text{nm/s}^{2}) with respect to time (s) cancels one power of seconds, leaving nm/s\text{nm/s} — a velocity. The answer is the motor's change in velocity over 0≤t≤10 \le t \le 1 s, not an acceleration.

      Sanity check on the sign: factor the integrand as a(t)=t2(6−7t)a(t) = t^{2}(6 - 7t). On [0,1][0,1] it is positive until t=6/7≈0.86t = 6/7 \approx 0.86 and only slightly negative after that, so the positive area dominates — a small positive answer is exactly what you should expect.
