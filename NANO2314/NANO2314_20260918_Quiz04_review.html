  1. 1. (1 pt) A plausible physical unit for the product rtrt (decay constant rr multiplied by time tt) in the exponential decay function e−rte^{-rt} is

    1. seconds
    2. inverse seconds
    3. nanometers
    4. inverse nanometers
    5. none of the above
    Solution for question 1

    Correct answer: E. none of the above

    Why: An exponent has to be a pure number — no unit at all. You can see why from the series in Question 6:

    e−rt=1−rt+12(rt)2−⋯e^{-rt} = 1 - rt + \tfrac{1}{2}(rt)^{2} - \cdots

    You cannot add the pure number 11 to something measured in seconds, so every power of rtrt — and therefore rtrt itself — must be dimensionless. None of A–D is dimensionless, so the answer is E.

    Watch out: choice A is the unit of tt alone; multiplying by rr is exactly what cancels the seconds. Choice B is the unit of rr alone (Question 2) — in the product the units cancel, (1/s)(s)=1(1/\text{s})(\text{s}) = 1. Choices C and D bring in a length, which nothing in a decay-in-time problem supplies; inverse nanometers would be the unit of a decay constant for decay in space, e−x/λe^{-x/\lambda}, and even there it is the constant, not the exponent, that carries it.

  2. 2. (1 pt) If time tt is measured in seconds, then the unit of rr in e−rte^{-rt} must be

    1. seconds
    2. inverse seconds
    3. nanometers
    4. inverse nanometers
    5. none of the above
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: B. inverse seconds

    Why: From Question 1, rtrt has no unit. If tt is in seconds, rr must cancel them:

    [r]=1[t]=s−1.[r] = \frac{1}{[t]} = \text{s}^{-1}.

    Read it as “the fraction lost per second.”

    Watch out: choice A would make rtrt come out in s2\text{s}^{2} — still not a pure number, which contradicts Question 1. Choice C is a length; rr sets how fast something decays in time. Choice D has the right form for a decay constant (one over the variable in the exponent) but the wrong variable — it goes with e−x/λe^{-x/\lambda}. Choice E: inverse seconds is listed, as B. Answering E here and E in Question 1 is worth a second look — it is precisely because rtrt has no unit that rr must carry the inverse of tt’s unit.

  3. 3. (1 pt) The decay e−rte^{-rt} can also be written as e−t/τe^{-t/\tau}, where τ\tau is called the decay time. If tt is measured in seconds, then the unit of τ\tau must be

    1. seconds
    2. inverse seconds
    3. nanometers
    4. inverse nanometers
    5. none of the above
    Solution for question 3

    Correct answer: A. seconds

    Why: Now the constant divides tt instead of multiplying it. For t/τt/\tau to be a pure number, τ\tau must have the same unit as tt. Comparing the two forms,

    e−rt=e−t/τ⇒τ=1r,1s−1=s.e^{-rt} = e^{-t/\tau} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau = \frac{1}{r}, \qquad \frac{1}{\text{s}^{-1}} = \text{s}.

    That is why τ\tau is called the decay time: after t=τt = \tau the quantity has fallen to 1/e≈37%1/e \approx 37\% of its starting value.

    Watch out: choice B is the unit of rr, carried over from Question 2 — the classic rr/τ\tau swap. Because τ=1/r\tau = 1/r, their units are reciprocals too. Choice C is a decay length, which belongs to e−x/λe^{-x/\lambda}. Choice D is wrong on both counts. Choice E: seconds is listed, as A. Of the three questions, this is the one where the constant has the same unit as tt.

  4. 4. (1 pt) In the function a+bzma + bz^{m}, the variable is zz; the letters aa, bb and mm are constants. Then ddz(a+bzm)\dfrac{d}{dz}\left(a + bz^{m}\right) is

    1. 00
    2. bzm−1b\,z^{m-1}
    3. mbzm−1m\,b\,z^{m-1}
    4. 1+mbzm−11 + m\,b\,z^{m-1}
    5. a+mbzm−1a + m\,b\,z^{m-1}
    Solution for question 4

    Correct answer: C. mbzm−1m\,b\,z^{m-1}

    Why: Treat every letter other than zz as a number. Then differentiate term by term:

    ddz(a)=0,ddz(bzm)=b⋅mzm−1.\frac{d}{dz}(a) = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dz}\left(bz^{m}\right) = b\cdot m\,z^{m-1}.

    The constant term disappears, the constant factor bb rides along unchanged, and the power rule brings the exponent mm down. This is exactly the skill used on 12r2t2\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}t^{2} in Question 6, where rr plays the role of bb.

    Watch out: choice E is the most common slip: the constant aa was carried through instead of differentiated — but ddz(a)=0\frac{d}{dz}(a) = 0. Choice D differentiates aa as if it were the variable (ddzz=1\frac{d}{dz}z = 1). Choice B lowers the exponent but forgets to bring it down as a factor. Choice A over-applies “constants differentiate to zero”: bzmbz^{m} genuinely depends on zz.

  5. 5. (2 pts) An object cools in surroundings held at the ambient temperature TaT_a (say Ta=76∘T_a = 76^{\circ}F). Its temperature TT at time tt is

    T=Ce−rt+Ta.T = Ce^{-rt} + T_a.

    1. Sketch this function for an object that starts hotter than its surroundings. Draw and label both axes (tt horizontal, TT vertical), and label TaT_a on your sketch. (ii) Explain why CC is not the temperature TT at t=0t = 0, and state what CC is instead.
    Solution for question 5

    (i) The sketch. The curve starts at T(0)=C+TaT(0) = C + T_a, above TaT_a for a hot object. It falls steeply at first and then more and more gently (decreasing, bending upward), and it levels off toward the horizontal line T=TaT = T_a — which it approaches but never crosses. Draw that line and label it TaT_a.

    What the sketch is saying: the part that decays is Ce−rtCe^{-rt}, the difference from the room temperature. That difference dies away to zero, leaving T→TaT \to T_a. A curve that decays toward the time axis (T→0T \to 0) would describe a room at 0∘0^{\circ}F.

    (ii) Why CC is not T(0)T(0). Set t=0t = 0, where e0=1e^{0} = 1:

    T(0)=C⋅1+Ta=C+Ta⇒C=T(0)−Ta.T(0) = C\cdot 1 + T_a = C + T_a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = T(0) - T_a.

    So CC is the initial temperature difference between the object and its surroundings — positive for an object that starts hotter and cools, negative for one that starts colder and warms. It equals T(0)T(0) only if Ta=0T_a = 0.

    Sanity check with units: e−rte^{-rt} is a pure number (Question 1), so CC must be a temperature, just like TT and TaT_a.

  6. 6. (4 pts) You are given

    e−rt=1−rt+12r2t2−16r3t3+⋯e^{-rt} = 1 - rt + \tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}t^{2} - \tfrac{1}{6}r^{3}t^{3} + \cdots

    1. (1.5 pts) Take the derivative with respect to time tt of each of the four terms 11, −rt-rt, 12r2t2\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}t^{2}, −16r3t3-\tfrac{1}{6}r^{3}t^{3}.

      Solution — part (a) of question 6

      Here rr is a constant — exactly like bb in Question 4 — so only the powers of tt change. Each drops by one and the old exponent comes down as a factor:

      ddt(1)=0,ddt(−rt)=−r,ddt(12r2t2)=r2t,ddt(−16r3t3)=−12r3t2.\frac{d}{dt}(1) = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}(-rt) = -r, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}t^{2}\right) = r^{2}t, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}r^{3}t^{3}\right) = -\tfrac{1}{2}r^{3}t^{2}.

      Keep the minus signs. The alternating signs are what make part (b) come out as −r-r rather than +r+r.

      Quick check: every power of tt went down by one, the constant vanished, and no power of rr changed.

    2. (1.5 pts) Use your answer from (a) to make a case that ddte−rt=−re−rt\dfrac{d}{dt}\,e^{-rt} = -r\,e^{-rt}.

      Solution — part (b) of question 6

      Add the four derivatives from (a) and factor out −r-r:

      0−r+r2t−12r3t2=−r[1−rt+12r2t2].0 - r + r^{2}t - \tfrac{1}{2}r^{3}t^{2} = -r\left[\,1 - rt + \tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}t^{2}\,\right].

      The bracket is exactly the beginning of the given series for e−rte^{-rt}. So differentiating the series gave back −r-r times the series: ddte−rt=−re−rt\dfrac{d}{dt}e^{-rt} = -r\,e^{-rt}.

      Why the bracket is one term short: the given series was cut off after the t3t^{3} term, and differentiating removes the constant. The next term, 124r4t4\tfrac{1}{24}r^{4}t^{4}, would differentiate to 16r4t3\tfrac{1}{6}r^{4}t^{3}, which is −r-r times the −16r3t3-\tfrac{1}{6}r^{3}t^{3} we already had. In general

      ddt(−rt)nn!=−r(−rt)n−1(n−1)!,\frac{d}{dt}\,\frac{(-rt)^{n}}{n!} = -r\,\frac{(-rt)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!},

      so every term becomes −r-r times the one before it, and the whole series reproduces itself times −r-r.

      Note that the question asks for a case built from (a). Quoting the chain rule states the result instead of showing it, and checking a single term with a number is an example, not a case.

    3. (1 pt) Using the result of (b), show that T=Ce−rt+TaT = Ce^{-rt} + T_a is a solution of the differential equation ddt[T−Ta]=−r[T−Ta]\dfrac{d}{dt}\left[T - T_a\right] = -r\left[T - T_a\right]. (Hint: recall that we defined ΔT=T−Ta\Delta T = T - T_a.)

      Solution — part (c) of question 6

      Show, don’t solve. You are handed a candidate TT; check that it satisfies the equation.

      Because TaT_a is a constant, subtracting it gives

      ΔT=T−Ta=Ce−rt.\Delta T = T - T_a = Ce^{-rt}.

      Differentiate, pulling the constant CC out and using (b):

      ddt[T−Ta]=Cddte−rt=C(−re−rt)=−r(Ce−rt)=−r[T−Ta].\frac{d}{dt}\left[T - T_a\right] = C\,\frac{d}{dt}e^{-rt} = C\left(-r\,e^{-rt}\right) = -r\left(Ce^{-rt}\right) = -r\left[T - T_a\right].

      The last step — recognizing Ce−rtCe^{-rt} as T−TaT - T_a again — is what closes the loop: the left side equals the right side, so T=Ce−rt+TaT = Ce^{-rt} + T_a is a solution.

      Equally good: differentiate TT directly. dTdt=−rCe−rt+0\frac{dT}{dt} = -rCe^{-rt} + 0, because TaT_a is constant, and since ddt[T−Ta]=dTdt\frac{d}{dt}[T - T_a] = \frac{dT}{dt}, the same rewriting finishes it.

      Reading it in words: the rate at which the temperature difference shrinks is proportional to the difference itself — the hotter the object compared with the room, the faster it cools. That is Newton’s law of cooling.

  7. 7. (1 pt) Extra credit. A cup of coffee at 196∘196^{\circ}F is set down in a room at Ta=76∘T_a = 76^{\circ}F. At the instant it is set down it is cooling at a rate of 6∘6^{\circ}F per minute. Using ddt[T−Ta]=−r[T−Ta]\dfrac{d}{dt}\left[T - T_a\right] = -r\left[T - T_a\right], find rr and give its unit. (The arithmetic works out without a calculator.)

    Solution for question 7

    Set up. Use the equation at the moment the cup is set down. The temperature difference then is

    [T−Ta]t=0=196−76=120∘F,\left[T - T_a\right]_{t=0} = 196 - 76 = 120^{\circ}\text{F},

    and it is shrinking at 6∘6^{\circ}F per minute, so ddt[T−Ta]=−6∘F/min\frac{d}{dt}[T - T_a] = -6^{\circ}\text{F/min} (negative, because it is cooling; TaT_a is constant, so the difference falls at the same rate as TT).

    Solve.

    −6∘Fmin=−r(120∘F)⇒r=6120min−1=120min−1=0.05min−1.-6\ \frac{^{\circ}\text{F}}{\text{min}} = -r\,(120^{\circ}\text{F}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{6}{120}\ \text{min}^{-1} = \frac{1}{20}\ \text{min}^{-1} = 0.05\ \text{min}^{-1}.

    Check the unit: the degrees cancel, leaving inverse minutes — an inverse time, exactly as Question 2 requires. (Equivalently 1/1200s−11/1200\ \text{s}^{-1}.) And rr comes out positive: the minus sign in the equation already says “decreasing.”

    Sanity check: τ=1/r=20\tau = 1/r = 20 minutes, so after about 20 minutes the 120∘120^{\circ}F gap has shrunk to about 37%37\% of itself — roughly 44∘44^{\circ}F above the room. Plausible for a cup of coffee.
