  1. 1. (1 pt) Questions 1–5 refer to the damped oscillation x(t)=Ae−γtcos⁡(ωt+δ),x(t) = A\,e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega t + \delta), where time tt is measured in seconds and the displacement xx is measured in mm.

    The appropriate unit for the decay rate γ\gamma is

    1. seconds
    2. inverse seconds
    3. mm
    4. inverse mm
    5. unitless
    Solution for question 1

    Correct answer: B. inverse seconds

    Why: The exponent γt\gamma t must be a pure number (Question 2), and tt is in seconds, so [γ]=1[t]=s−1.[\gamma] = \frac{1}{[t]} = \text{s}^{-1}. It is the same as rr in e−rte^{-rt} on Quiz 4.

    Watch out: Choice A is the unit of tt — and of the decay time τ=1/γ\tau = 1/\gamma — not of the rate; it would make γt\gamma t come out in s2\text{s}^{2}, which cannot sit in an exponent. Choice C: the length lives in the amplitude AA, outside the exponential. Choice D has the right form for a decay constant (one over the variable in the exponent) but the wrong variable: inverse mm would go with decay in space, e−x/λe^{-x/\lambda}. Choice E: only the product γt\gamma t is unitless; γ\gamma itself has to cancel the second.

  2. 2. (1 pt) The appropriate unit for the product γt\gamma t in e−γte^{-\gamma t} is

    1. seconds
    2. inverse seconds
    3. mm
    4. inverse mm
    5. unitless
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: E. unitless

    Why: An exponent must be a pure number. From the series e−γt=1−γt+12(γt)2−⋯e^{-\gamma t} = 1 - \gamma t + \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma t)^{2} - \cdots you cannot add the pure number 11 to something measured in seconds. In the product the seconds cancel: (1/s)(s)=1(1/\text{s})(\text{s}) = 1. Same idea as rtrt on Quiz 4 and ωt\omega t on Quiz 5.

    Watch out: Choice A is the unit of tt alone and Choice B the unit of γ\gamma alone (Question 1); multiplying them is exactly what removes the second. Choices C and D are lengths, and nothing in the exponent involves a length — the mm lives in AA.

  3. 3. (1 pt) Which of the following is a widely used “unit” that is actually unitless?

    1. seconds
    2. inverse seconds
    3. mm
    4. inverse mm
    5. radians
    Solution for question 3

    Correct answer: E. radians

    Why: A radian is arc length divided by radius (mm/mm), a pure number. That is why the cosine’s argument ωt+δ\omega t + \delta can be “in radians” and still be dimensionless, and why rad/s\text{rad/s} is the same as s−1\text{s}^{-1}.

    Watch out: Choices A–D are all real dimensions: time, inverse time, length and inverse length.

  4. 4. (1 pt) The parameter ω\omega is called ________ and its unit should be ________.

    1. period; inverse seconds
    2. phase constant; seconds
    3. frequency; unitless
    4. angular frequency; radians per second
    5. phase constant; radians
    Solution for question 4

    Correct answer: D. angular frequency; radians per second

    Why: ωt\omega t must be an angle in radians and tt is in seconds, so [ω]=rads(=s−1).[\omega] = \frac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \;(= \text{s}^{-1}). ω\omega is the angular frequency, ω=2πf=2π/T\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/T. Same as Quiz 5, Question 2.

    Watch out: Choice A: ω\omega is not the period (ω=2π/T\omega = 2\pi/T), and a period is measured in seconds, not inverse seconds. Choices B and E describe δ\delta (Question 5). Choice C: the ordinary frequency is f=1/Tf = 1/T; ω\omega is the angular frequency, and neither is unitless — a unitless ω\omega would leave seconds in ωt\omega t.

  5. 5. (1 pt) The parameter δ\delta is called ________ and its unit should be ________.

    1. period; inverse seconds
    2. phase constant; seconds
    3. frequency; unitless
    4. angular frequency; radians per second
    5. phase constant; radians
    Solution for question 5

    Correct answer: E. phase constant; radians

    Why: δ\delta is added to the angle ωt\omega t inside the cosine, so it is itself an angle, in radians. It is the phase constant: it sets where in the cycle the oscillation is at t=0t = 0, since x(0)=Acos⁡δx(0) = A\cos\delta.

    Watch out: Choice B has the right name but the wrong unit: only like quantities can be added, and radians plus seconds has no meaning. Choice A: δ\delta is neither a time nor an inverse time. Choice C: δ\delta does not set how fast the motion repeats. Choice D describes ω\omega (Question 4).

  6. 6. (5 pts) A cold drink sits in a room with an ambient temperature of Ta=76∘T_a = 76^{\circ}F. Initially (at t=0t = 0), the drink’s temperature TT is 36∘36^{\circ}F. The drink’s temperature as a function of time, T(t)T(t), follows the first-order differential equation ddt[T−Ta]=−r[T−Ta],\frac{d}{dt}\left[T - T_a\right] = -\,r\left[T - T_a\right], where r>0r > 0 is the decay rate.

    1. (1 pt) In the situation described here, the sign of T−TaT - T_a is (fill in one bubble) A. positive, B. negative, C. undefined.

      Solution — part (a) of question 6

      Negative — B. At t=0t = 0, T−Ta=36−76=−40∘T - T_a = 36 - 76 = -40^{\circ}F. The drink warms toward 76∘76^{\circ}F but never passes it, so T−TaT - T_a stays negative the whole time.

    2. (2 pts) Sketch T−TaT - T_a vs. tt (not TT vs. tt). Label CC = the initial temperature deviation T−TaT - T_a at t=0t = 0.

      Solution — part (b) of question 6

      Put T−TaT - T_a on the vertical axis and tt on the horizontal axis. The curve starts below the tt-axis at C=T(0)−Ta=−40∘FC = T(0) - T_a = -40^{\circ}\text{F} and rises toward 00 — steepest at t=0t = 0, then flattening out — approaching the tt-axis without ever crossing it. Label CC at that starting point. This is the mirror image of Quiz 4’s hot object, where CC was positive and the curve fell toward 00 from above. Two common slips: plotting TT itself (rising from 36 to 76), and calling CC the initial temperature T0=36∘T_0 = 36^{\circ}F — CC is the initial deviation from room temperature.

    3. (2 pts) Write a legitimate solution of the differential equation above, and verify it by substitution and differentiation.

      Solution — part (c) of question 6

      A solution: T−Ta=Ce−rt,i.e.T(t)=Ta+Ce−rt,T - T_a = C\,e^{-rt}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad T(t) = T_a + C\,e^{-rt}, with any constant CC (for this drink C=−40∘C = -40^{\circ}F, so T=76−40e−rtT = 76 - 40\,e^{-rt}).

      Verify. Left side: ddt[Ce−rt]=−rCe−rt.\frac{d}{dt}\left[C e^{-rt}\right] = -r\,C e^{-rt}. Right side: −r[T−Ta]=−rCe−rt.-r\left[T - T_a\right] = -r\,C e^{-rt}. The two sides are equal, so the solution is legitimate. A verification has to differentiate: writing the equation again, or plugging in numbers at t=0t = 0, does not check anything. And T=Ce−rtT = C e^{-rt} on its own is not a solution for TT — the TaT_a is what makes the drink approach room temperature.

  7. 7. (6 pts) Just as erte^{rt} can be written as ert=1+(rt)+(rt)2/2!+(rt)3/3!+(rt)4/4!+⋯e^{rt} = 1 + (rt) + (rt)^{2}/2! + (rt)^{3}/3! + (rt)^{4}/4! + \cdots, cos⁡(ωt)\cos(\omega t) can be written as cos⁡(ωt)=1−(ωt)22!+(ωt)44!−(ωt)66!+⋯\cos(\omega t) = 1 - \frac{(\omega t)^{2}}{2!} + \frac{(\omega t)^{4}}{4!} - \frac{(\omega t)^{6}}{6!} + \cdots

    1. (2 pts) Take the first derivative with respect to time tt of each of the four terms 1,−(ωt)22!,(ωt)44!,−(ωt)66!.1, \qquad -\frac{(\omega t)^{2}}{2!}, \qquad \frac{(\omega t)^{4}}{4!}, \qquad -\frac{(\omega t)^{6}}{6!}.

      Solution — part (a) of question 7

      Expand each power, (ωt)n=ωntn(\omega t)^{n} = \omega^{n}t^{n}, and use the power rule with every letter except tt held constant: ddt1=0,ddt[−ω2t22!]=−2ω2t2!=−ω2t,\frac{d}{dt}1 = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left[-\frac{\omega^{2}t^{2}}{2!}\right] = -\frac{2\,\omega^{2}t}{2!} = -\omega^{2}t, ddt[ω4t44!]=4ω4t34!=ω4t33!,ddt[−ω6t66!]=−6ω6t56!=−ω6t55!.\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{\omega^{4}t^{4}}{4!}\right] = \frac{4\,\omega^{4}t^{3}}{4!} = \frac{\omega^{4}t^{3}}{3!}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left[-\frac{\omega^{6}t^{6}}{6!}\right] = -\frac{6\,\omega^{6}t^{5}}{6!} = -\frac{\omega^{6}t^{5}}{5!}. Watch the ω\omega’s: (ωt)2=ω2t2(\omega t)^{2} = \omega^{2}t^{2}, so its derivative is 2ω2t2\omega^{2}t, not 2ωt2\omega t. And bring the exponent down: the derivative of ω4t4/4!\omega^{4}t^{4}/4! is not ω4t3/4!\omega^{4}t^{3}/4!. Unsimplified factorials (4ω4t3/244\omega^{4}t^{3}/24) are perfectly fine.

    2. (2 pts) Take the second derivative with respect to tt of each of the same four terms.

      Solution — part (b) of question 7

      Differentiate the results of (a) once more: 0,−ω2,3ω4t23!=ω4t22!,−5ω6t45!=−ω6t44!.0, \qquad -\omega^{2}, \qquad \frac{3\,\omega^{4}t^{2}}{3!} = \frac{\omega^{4}t^{2}}{2!}, \qquad -\frac{5\,\omega^{6}t^{4}}{5!} = -\frac{\omega^{6}t^{4}}{4!}.

    3. (2 pts) Using your answer from (b), make a case that d2dt2cos⁡(ωt)=−ω2cos⁡(ωt)\dfrac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\cos(\omega t) = -\,\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t).

      Solution — part (c) of question 7

      Add the results of (b) and factor out −ω2-\omega^{2}: −ω2+ω4t22!−ω6t44!+⋯=−ω2[1−(ωt)22!+(ωt)44!−⋯].-\omega^{2} + \frac{\omega^{4}t^{2}}{2!} - \frac{\omega^{6}t^{4}}{4!} + \cdots = -\omega^{2}\left[1 - \frac{(\omega t)^{2}}{2!} + \frac{(\omega t)^{4}}{4!} - \cdots\right]. The bracket is the series for cos⁡(ωt)\cos(\omega t), so d2dt2cos⁡(ωt)=−ω2cos⁡(ωt).\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\cos(\omega t) = -\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t). Four terms in give three non-zero terms out, because the constant 11 differentiates to 00; the next term of the series, +(ωt)8/8!+(\omega t)^{8}/8!, supplies the next bracket term, so the pattern continues forever. The chain rule (cos⁡→−ωsin⁡→−ω2cos\cos \to -\omega\sin \to -\omega^{2}\cos) gives the same answer, but the question asks you to build the case from your (b) — that is the point of the series argument (Quizzes 3 and 4).

  8. 8. (4 pts) You’re supervising an inexperienced intern named Urotas Irome, who is quantifying the stiffness kk of a cantilever. Urotas has measured the oscillation period TT for 6 different samples with different masses. The dynamics of the cantilever is approximated by the undamped harmonic oscillator (a second-order differential equation without a damping term) d2dt2z(t)=−ω2z(t),\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\,z(t) = -\,\omega^{2}\,z(t), where ω2=k/m\omega^{2} = k/m, and mm is the mass of the sample cantilever.

    1. (2 pts) As usual, ω=2π/T\omega = 2\pi/T. What is TT in terms of kk and mm?

      Solution — part (a) of question 8

      T=2πω=2πk/m=2πmk.T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{k/m}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}. One over the square root of a fraction is the square root of the flipped fraction. Check it: a heavier sample swings more slowly (longer TT, as in Urotas’s graph), and a stiffer cantilever faster. It is the twin of the pendulum’s T=2πL/gT = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g} from Quiz 5.

    2. (2 pts) Urotas has given you a graph of TT (vertical axis) against mm (horizontal axis): six data points at m=1,2,…,6m = 1, 2, \ldots, 6 with error bars, a square-root fit curve through them, tick numbers on both axes but no units, and a legend reading “Fit result: k=6.762026767k = 6.762026767”. What should you tell or advise Urotas?

      Solution — part (b) of question 8

      Four things are wrong with the report:

      1. The vertical axis TT has no unit (seconds? microseconds?).
      2. The horizontal axis mm has no unit (kg? ng?).
      3. The fit result kk has no unit.
      4. k=6.762026767k = 6.762026767 has ten significant figures and no uncertainty, although the data scatter and carry error bars of several percent. Report the fit uncertainty and round to it — something like k=6.8±0.3k = 6.8 \pm 0.3, in its unit.

      What is fine: the data do follow the m\sqrt{m} trend, and the points carry error bars. “The fit looks good” is not advice: the problems are the missing units and the unjustified precision. For reference, one consistent set of units is TT in μ\mus, mm in ng and kk in N/m. The unit of kk is Question 9.

  9. 9. (1.5 pts) Extra credit. What could be an appropriate unit for the stiffness kk in Question 8?

    Solution for question 9

    From ω2=k/m\omega^{2} = k/m: k=mω2,[k]=kg⋅s−2=kg/s2.k = m\,\omega^{2}, \qquad [k] = \text{kg}\cdot\text{s}^{-2} = \text{kg/s}^{2}. Since N=kgm/s2\text{N} = \text{kg}\,\text{m/s}^{2}, this is the same as N/m\text{N/m} — force per unit deflection, as in Hooke’s law F=−kzF = -kz. Any mass unit over seconds squared (such as g/s2\text{g/s}^{2}) is an appropriate unit too. AFM data sheets quote cantilever stiffness in N/m (equivalently nN/nm).

  10. 10. (1.5 pts) Extra credit. Consider the differential equation d2dt2x(t)=−ω2x(t).\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\,x(t) = -\,\omega^{2}\,x(t). Show that x(t)=Acos⁡(ωt)+x0x(t) = A\cos(\omega t) + x_{0}, with x0≠0x_{0} \neq 0, is NOT a legitimate solution (e.g., by substitution and differentiation). How can you modify the differential equation to make it a legitimate solution?

    Solution for question 10

    Substitute. Left side — the constant x0x_{0} differentiates to 00: d2dt2[Acos(ωt)+x0]=−Aω2cos⁡(ωt).\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\left[A\cos(\omega t) + x_{0}\right] = -A\,\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t). Right side — the x0x_{0} stays: −ω2[Acos(ωt)+x0]=−Aω2cos⁡(ωt)−ω2x0.-\omega^{2}\left[A\cos(\omega t) + x_{0}\right] = -A\,\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t) - \omega^{2}x_{0}. The two sides differ by ω2x0≠0\omega^{2}x_{0} \neq 0, so it is not a solution. (x0x_{0} drops out of the left side only — not out of both.)

    Fix the equation, not the solution: measure the displacement from x0x_{0} — the same move as T−TaT - T_a in Question 6: d2dt2[x−x0]=−ω2[x−x0],i.e.d2xdt2=−ω2(x−x0)=−ω2x+ω2x0.\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\left[x - x_{0}\right] = -\omega^{2}\left[x - x_{0}\right], \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}} = -\omega^{2}\left(x - x_{0}\right) = -\omega^{2}x + \omega^{2}x_{0}. Now both sides equal −Aω2cos⁡(ωt)-A\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t). Physically, the oscillation is about an equilibrium at x0x_{0} instead of 00 — a mass hanging on a vertical spring, for example. Note the coefficient: the added term is ω2x0\omega^{2}x_{0}, not just x0x_{0}.

Where the class had the most trouble

These cost the most points. If you lost points here, start your review with them.

  1. Question 8(b) — the missing units. Everyone caught that k=6.762026767k = 6.762026767 has far too many digits, and most said kk has no unit, but only about a third noticed that neither axis has a unit. A graph whose axes have no units cannot be read: TT in seconds and TT in microseconds are very different experiments. This is the same lesson as the intern on Quiz 5.
  2. Question 6(b) — plotting T−TaT - T_a, not TT. Every bubble in 6(a) was right (T−TaT - T_a is negative), yet several sketches showed TT rising from 36 to 76, or a curve above the axis. If T−TaT - T_a is negative, its graph lives below the tt-axis, and CC is the starting value there, −40∘-40^{\circ}F — not the starting temperature.
  3. Question 10 — fixing the equation. Most who tried showed that x0x_{0} breaks the equation, but only one wrote a correct modified equation. Adding x0x_{0} alone is not enough — the term is ω2x0\omega^{2}x_{0} — and changing the solution (dropping x0x_{0}, adding a phase constant) does not answer the question.
  4. Question 7 — the details of the derivatives. Hold ω\omega constant and keep it: (ωt)2(\omega t)^{2} is ω2t2\omega^{2}t^{2}. Bring the exponent down, keep the minus signs, and remember that the derivative of the constant 11 is 00. In (c), factor −ω2-\omega^{2} out of your (b) — the chain rule alone does not use the series.
  5. Question 6(c) — a solution is a function of tt. Plugging numbers in at t=0t = 0 gives one value, not a solution, and a verification has to differentiate.
