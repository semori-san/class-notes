Quiz 5 — September 28, 2026

  1. 1. (1 pt) A plausible physical unit for the product ωt\omega t (angular frequency ω\omega multiplied by time tt) in the pendulum solution x(t)=Acos⁡(ωt)x(t) = A\cos(\omega t) is

    1. seconds
    2. inverse seconds
    3. nanometers
    4. inverse nanometers
    5. none of the above
    Solution for question 1

    Correct answer: E. none of the above

    Why: ωt\omega t is the argument of the cosine, so it must be a pure number — an angle in radians, and a radian is dimensionless (arc length divided by radius). You can see why from the series

    cos⁡θ=1−12θ2+⋯\cos\theta = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^{2} + \cdots

    You cannot subtract θ2\theta^{2} from the pure number 11 unless θ\theta carries no unit. None of A–D is dimensionless, so the answer is E. It is the same idea as rtrt in e−rte^{-rt} on Quiz 4.

    Watch out: Choice A is the unit of tt alone; multiplying by ω\omega is exactly what removes the second. Choice B is the unit of ω\omega alone (Question 2) — in the product the seconds cancel, (1/s)(s)=1(1/\text{s})(\text{s}) = 1. Choice C is a length: in x(t)=Acos⁡(ωt)x(t) = A\cos(\omega t) the length lives in the amplitude AA, outside the cosine. Choice D is the unit of a wavenumber kk in cos⁡(kx)\cos(kx) for oscillation in space — and even there it is kk, not the product kxkx, that carries it.

  2. 2. (1 pt) If time tt is measured in seconds, then ω\omega should be measured in

    1. seconds
    2. seconds per radian
    3. radians per second
    4. degrees
    5. inverse degrees
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: C. radians per second

    Why: ωt\omega t must be an angle in radians (Question 1) and tt is in seconds, so

    [ω]=rads.[\omega] = \frac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}}.

    Since a radian is dimensionless, this is the same as s−1\text{s}^{-1}, which is how the homework wrote it. Check with ω=2π/T\omega = 2\pi/T: 2π2\pi radians (one full cycle) per period.

    Watch out: Choice A would make ωt\omega t come out in s2\text{s}^{2} — not dimensionless, which contradicts Question 1. Choice B is upside down: it is the unit of 1/ω1/\omega. ω\omega tells you how much angle is swept per second. Choice D has no time in it — that is the unit of the whole phase ωt\omega t if you measured angles in degrees, not of ω\omega; and the calculus we use, ddtcos⁡(ωt)=−ωsin⁡(ωt)\tfrac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega t) = -\omega\sin(\omega t), only works in radians. Choice E has no time dependence at all and is inverted as well.

  3. 3. (1 pt) In a+bx+cyma + bx + cy^{m}, the variable is yy; aa, bb, cc, mm and xx are all constants. Then ddy(a+bx+cym)\dfrac{d}{dy}\left(a + bx + cy^{m}\right) is

    1. 00
    2. mcym−1m\,c\,y^{m-1}
    3. b+mcym−1b + m\,c\,y^{m-1}
    4. cym−1c\,y^{m-1}
    5. a+bx+mcym−1a + bx + m\,c\,y^{m-1}
    Solution for question 3

    Correct answer: B. mcym−1m\,c\,y^{m-1}

    Why: Treat every letter other than yy as a number and differentiate term by term:

    ddy(a)=0,ddy(bx)=0,ddy(cym)=mcym−1.\frac{d}{dy}(a) = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dy}(bx) = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dy}\left(cy^{m}\right) = m\,c\,y^{m-1}.

    bxbx is a product of two constants, so it is just a constant. The constant factor cc rides along, and the power rule brings the exponent mm down.

    Watch out: Choice C is the point of the question: bxbx was differentiated as if xx were the variable (ddx(bx)=b\tfrac{d}{dx}(bx) = b). But the derivative is with respect to yy, and here xx is a constant. The letter xx does not have to be the variable. Choice E carries the constant terms through instead of differentiating them — the same slip as Quiz 4, Question 4. Choice D lowers the exponent but forgets to bring it down as a factor. Choice A over-applies “constants differentiate to zero”: cymcy^{m} genuinely depends on yy.

  4. 4. (1 pt) For a pendulum of length LL under gravitational acceleration gg, the period TT can be written as

    Hint: think back to the experiment. How did TT depend on LL — did a longer LL give you a shorter or a longer TT?

    1. g/L\sqrt{g/L}
    2. 2πg/L2\pi\sqrt{g/L}
    3. gL\sqrt{gL}
    4. 2πg/L2\pi\, g/L
    5. 2πL/g2\pi\sqrt{L/g}
    Solution for question 4

    Correct answer: E. 2πL/g2\pi\sqrt{L/g}

    Why: In the experiment a longer pendulum swung more slowly: T∝LT \propto \sqrt{L}, which is why TT against L\sqrt{L} plotted as a straight line through the origin. Only LL in the numerator does that. The unit confirms it:

    mm/s2=s2=s.\sqrt{\frac{\text{m}}{\text{m/s}^{2}}} = \sqrt{\text{s}^{2}} = \text{s}.

    And stronger gravity gives a shorter period, as it should.

    Watch out: Choice A is ω\omega, not TT (Question 5): its unit is s−1\text{s}^{-1}, and it shrinks as LL grows — the opposite of what you measured. Choice B swaps LL and gg: again s−1\text{s}^{-1}, and a longer pendulum would swing faster. Choice C grows with LL, but its unit is m/s\text{m/s}, a speed, and it would make the period longer under stronger gravity. Choice D has unit s−2\text{s}^{-2} and decreases with LL — wrong on both counts.

  5. 5. (2 pts) Using your answer to Question 4 and the definition ω=2π/T\omega = 2\pi/T, find ω2\omega^{2} in terms of gg and LL. This is the ω2\omega^{2} that appears in the second-order differential equation

    d2dt2x(t)=−ω2x(t).\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\,x(t) = -\,\omega^{2}\,x(t).

    Solution for question 5

    Substitute the period from Question 4 into the definition:

    ω=2πT=2π2πL/g.\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi\sqrt{L/g}}.

    Simplify. The 2π2\pi cancels, and one over the square root of a fraction is the square root of the flipped fraction:

    ω=1L/g=gL.\omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L/g}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}.

    Square:

    ω2=gL,sod2dt2x(t)=−gLx(t).\omega^{2} = \frac{g}{L}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\,x(t) = -\frac{g}{L}\,x(t).

    Equally good: square first. ω2=4π2/T2=4π2/(4π2L/g)=g/L\omega^{2} = 4\pi^{2}/T^{2} = 4\pi^{2}/\left(4\pi^{2}L/g\right) = g/L.

    Sanity check with units: [g/L]=(m/s2)/m=s−2[g/L] = (\text{m/s}^{2})/\text{m} = \text{s}^{-2}, so ω\omega is in s−1\text{s}^{-1}, consistent with Question 2. It also matches the homework, where the same small-angle equation gave x(t)=Acos⁡(g/Lt+ϕ)x(t) = A\cos\!\left(\sqrt{g/L}\,t + \phi\right).

    Three slips to avoid: multiplying instead of dividing (2π⋅2πL/g2\pi \cdot 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}); forgetting to flip the fraction when inverting the root (ω2=L/g\omega^{2} = L/g — check the unit: s2\text{s}^{2}, not s−2\text{s}^{-2}); and stopping at ω=g/L\omega = \sqrt{g/L} when the question asks for ω2\omega^{2}.

  6. 6. (2 pts) Sketch a valid solution x(t)x(t) of the second-order differential equation d2dt2x(t)=−ω2x(t)\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\,x(t) = -\,\omega^{2}\,x(t) (for a pendulum or oscillator starting at maximum displacement at t=0t = 0, as announced in class), with the displacement xx on the vertical axis and time tt on the horizontal axis. Label both axes. On your graph, indicate the amplitude AA and the period TT.

    Solution for question 6

    The curve. Starting at maximum displacement means starting at a crest: x(0)=Ax(0) = A. From there it is a smooth wave that swings evenly above and below x=0x = 0, with the same height every cycle, for at least one full cycle. That is

    x(t)=Acos⁡(ωt),ω=g/L (Question 5),x(t) = A\cos(\omega t), \qquad \omega = \sqrt{g/L} \text{ (Question 5)},

    a cosine, not a sine: a sine starts at x=0x = 0. (Starting at a trough, x(0)=−Ax(0) = -A, is also maximum displacement.)

    Amplitude AA: the distance from the center line x=0x = 0 to a crest (or to a trough). It is half the crest-to-trough height; crest to trough is 2A2A.

    Period TT: the time for one complete cycle — crest to the next crest, trough to the next trough, or from one zero crossing to the next one in the same direction. Crest to trough, or between two neighbouring zero crossings, is only T/2T/2.

    Check that it solves the equation: differentiating twice, d2dt2Acos⁡(ωt)=−ω2Acos⁡(ωt)=−ω2x(t)\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}A\cos(\omega t) = -\omega^{2}A\cos(\omega t) = -\omega^{2}x(t). A slowly shrinking oscillation is how a real pendulum behaves, but it is not a solution of this equation — the equation has no friction in it.

  7. 7. (2 pts) You supervise an inexperienced intern who is quantifying the gravitational acceleration on Planet 67 (pronounced “six-seven”), whose gravity is somewhat smaller than Earth’s. Your intern conducted an experiment on 5 different pendulums and reported the gravitational acceleration to be 6.76720256.7672025. What would you tell your intern?

    Solution for question 7

    Two things are wrong with the report:

    1. No unit. “6.76720256.7672025” is just a number. An acceleration needs its unit, m/s2\text{m/s}^{2}. Without it the result is meaningless — in ft/s2\text{ft/s}^{2} it would describe a very different planet.

    2. Far too many digits, and no error bar. Eight significant figures claims the result is known to about one part in a hundred million. Five hand-timed pendulums cannot support that: a reaction-time error of about 0.10.1 s in TT is a percent-level error, and it enters g∝1/T2g \propto 1/T^{2} doubled. Report the uncertainty from the fit (or the standard error σg/N\sigma_g/\sqrt{N} with N=5N = 5) and round gg to match it — something like

    g=6.8±0.2m/s2.g = 6.8 \pm 0.2\ \text{m/s}^{2}.

    In the homework the fit was reported to 0.1m/s20.1\ \text{m/s}^{2}.

    What is fine: the value is plausibly below Earth’s 9.8m/s29.8\ \text{m/s}^{2}, as it should be. That is a sanity check on the number, not a criticism of the report. Suggesting more trials is good advice, but it does not fix either problem: even with 25 pendulums, the report would still need a unit and an honest number of digits.

Where the class had the most trouble

These four cost the most points. If you lost points here, start your review with them.

  1. Question 7 — the missing unit. Almost everyone (12 of 14) spotted that 6.76720256.7672025 is far too precise, but only 2 of 14 pointed out that it has no unit. A measured quantity is not a result until it has a unit (m/s2\text{m/s}^{2}) and an uncertainty. Writing m/s2\text{m/s}^{2} yourself is not the same as telling the intern it is missing.
  2. Question 6 — marking the amplitude. Only 5 of 14 marked AA correctly. AA is the distance from the center line x=0x = 0 to a crest (or trough). Crest to trough is 2A2A, and an arrow that just points at a crest does not show a distance.
  3. Question 5 — reaching ω2=g/L\omega^{2} = g/L. Half the class did not get to the final result. The usual slips: 1/L/g1/\sqrt{L/g} is g/L\sqrt{g/L} (flip the fraction when you invert a root), the question asks for ω2\omega^{2} (square it), and a wrong period from Question 4 carries through — 4 of 14 missed Question 4. A unit check catches all of these: g/Lg/L is in s−2\text{s}^{-2}, L/gL/g in s2\text{s}^{2}.
  4. Question 6 — what the graph shows. Several sketches did not swing evenly about x=0x = 0 (the whole wave sat above or below the tt-axis, more like cos⁡2\cos^{2} than cos\cos), and 5 of 14 did not mark TT as one full cycle. The solution of ẍ=−ω2x\ddot{x} = -\omega^{2}x oscillates about equilibrium, x=0x = 0, and TT runs from a crest to the next crest.
