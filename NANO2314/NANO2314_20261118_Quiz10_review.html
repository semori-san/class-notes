  1. 1. (2 pts) The eigenvalue equation below has a diagonal 4×4 matrix. (6000070000200004)(c1c2c3c4)=λ(c1c2c3c4)\begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_{1} \\ c_{2} \\ c_{3} \\ c_{4} \end{pmatrix} = \lambda \begin{pmatrix} c_{1} \\ c_{2} \\ c_{3} \\ c_{4} \end{pmatrix}

    1. (1 pt) What are the eigenvalues λ\lambda of the 4×4 matrix? Briefly justify.

      Solution — part (a) of question 1

      multiply out row 1: 6c1+0c2+0c3+0c4=λc1⇒6c1=λc16c_{1} + 0c_{2} + 0c_{3} + 0c_{4} = \lambda c_{1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 6c_{1} = \lambda c_{1}

      ↓ off-diagonal zeros: each row has one cc

      7c2=λc2,2c3=λc3,4c4=λc47c_{2} = \lambda c_{2}, \qquad 2c_{3} = \lambda c_{3}, \qquad 4c_{4} = \lambda c_{4}

      Why: a nonzero c1c_{1} forces λ=6\lambda = 6; a nonzero c2c_{2} forces λ=7\lambda = 7, and so on. The rows don’t mix.

      Conclusion

      λ=6,7,2,4\lambda = 6,\ 7,\ 2,\ 4: the diagonal entries

      In words. In a diagonal matrix the off-diagonal entries are 0, so each row of the equation reads (diagonal entry) × c = λ × c. The eigenvalues are just the numbers on the diagonal.

    2. (1 pt) Fill in each λ\lambda and write its eigenvector below it. Full credit for normalized eigenvectors.

      Solution — part (b) of question 1

      λ=6\lambda = 6: rows 2–4 read 7c2=6c27c_{2} = 6c_{2}, 2c3=6c32c_{3} = 6c_{3}, 4c4=6c44c_{4} = 6c_{4}, so c2=c3=c4=0c_{2} = c_{3} = c_{4} = 0

      ↓ only c1c_{1} survives; pick c1=1c_{1} = 1

      Conclusion

      λ=6\lambda = 6: (1000)\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, λ=7\lambda = 7: (0100)\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, λ=2\lambda = 2: (0010)\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, λ=4\lambda = 4: (0001)\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}

      Why: each has length 12=1\sqrt{1^{2}} = 1, so they are already normalized. (5,0,0,0)(5, 0, 0, 0) is also an eigenvector for λ=6\lambda = 6, but not a normalized one. The eigenvalue itself (6) is not the eigenvector.

      In words. Each eigenvector has a single 1, in the row of its eigenvalue, and 0 everywhere else. A vector with a single 1 already has length 1.

  2. 2. (1 pt) For an electron in a molecule, what two things do we want to find by solving the Schrödinger equation?

    1. The population of electron spins, and the nuclear resonance frequency
    2. The probability of finding the atoms, and the distances between them
    3. The allowed energies, and the wavefunctions (orbitals) of the electron
    4. The exact position of the electron, and its energy levels
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: C. The allowed energies, and the wavefunctions (orbitals) of the electron

    Why: Solving Ĥψ=Eψ\hat{H}\psi = E\psi is an eigenvalue problem: its eigenvalues EE are the allowed energies, and its eigenvectors (eigenfunctions) ψ\psi are the wavefunctions — the orbitals. In the Hückel model these are the energy levels and the vectors of cc’s.

    Watch out: Choice D is half right: the energy levels, yes, but the electron has no exact position. The wavefunction gives only the probability of finding it, |ψ|2|\psi|^{2}. Choice B is about the atoms: in a molecular-orbital calculation they are held fixed, and we solve for the electron. Choice A (spins, nuclear resonance) is not what the Schrödinger equation for the electron gives.

  3. 3. (7 pts) HH below is the Hückel Hamiltonian of a conjugated hydrocarbon (α,β>0\alpha, \beta > 0). H=(−α−β0−β−β−α−β00−β−α−β−β0−β−α)H = \begin{pmatrix} -\alpha & -\beta & 0 & -\beta \\ -\beta & -\alpha & -\beta & 0 \\ 0 & -\beta & -\alpha & -\beta \\ -\beta & 0 & -\beta & -\alpha \end{pmatrix}

    1. (1.5 pts) How many unsaturated carbon atoms does the molecule have? Sketch the molecule.

      Solution — part (a) of question 3

      HH is 4×4: one row per carbon 2pz2p_{z} orbital, so 4 unsaturated carbons

      ↓ next-door carbons share a −β-\beta

      −β-\beta next to the diagonal bonds C1–C2, C2–C3, C3–C4, and the corners H14=H41=−βH_{14} = H_{41} = -\beta bond C4 back to C1

      Why: a chain (butadiene) would have 0 in those two corners. The corner entries close the ring.

      Conclusion

      a ring of four carbons: cyclobutadiene, C₄H₄, alternating double bonds, one H on each C

      In words. Four rows means four carbons. The −β in the top-right and bottom-left corners connects carbon 4 to carbon 1, so the four carbons form a ring, not a chain.

    2. (2.5 pts) The Hückel solver’s output for HH is below (a=αa = \alpha, b=βb = \beta).

      Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors:
Eigenvalue: -a (multiplicity 2)
  Eigenvector: [-1.000, 0, 1.000, 0]
  Eigenvector: [0, -1.000, 0, 1.000]
Eigenvalue: -a - 2.0*b (multiplicity 1)
  Eigenvector: [1.000, 1.000, 1.000, 1.000]
Eigenvalue: -a + 2.0*b (multiplicity 1)
  Eigenvector: [-1.000, 1.000, -1.000, 1.000]

      With −α=−1-\alpha = -1 eV and −β=−0.5-\beta = -0.5 eV, sketch the energy levels and fill them with the π\pi electrons.

      Solution — part (b) of question 3

      put in a=1a = 1 eV, b=0.5b = 0.5 eV: −a−2b=−2eV,−a=−1eV(multiplicity 2),−a+2b=0eV-a - 2b = -2\ \text{eV}, \qquad -a = -1\ \text{eV}\ (\text{multiplicity } 2), \qquad -a + 2b = 0\ \text{eV}

      ↓ multiplicity 2: two separate levels

      sort by energy: −2-2 at the bottom, the pair at −1-1, 00 at the top

      ↓ 4 π electrons, filled from the bottom

      Conclusion

      a pair at −2 eV, one electron in each −1 eV level, 0 eV empty

      Why: the solver lists the levels in its own order, not by energy. A pair in one −1 eV level also earned full credit.

      In words. Plug in the numbers, draw each level at its height on the axis (two lines at −1 eV), and fill the four π electrons from the bottom up, at most two per level.

    3. (2 pts) Sketch each molecular orbital below its eigenvector and write its number of nodes.

      Solution — part (c) of question 3

      put each coefficient on its carbon, around the ring: C1 is next to C2 and C4; C1 and C3 sit across the ring

      ↓ filled top lobe +, open −, a dot for 0

      count the sign changes going all the way around, including C4 back to C1

      Why: [−1,1,−1,1][-1, 1, -1, 1] changes sign four times around the ring (2 nodal lines). On a chain it would be three — but this molecule is a ring.

      Conclusion

      nodes: 0, 1, 1, 2 nodal lines (or 0, 2, 2, 4 nodal points on the ring); more nodes, higher energy

      In words. Draw the orbitals on the ring, not on a chain, leave a carbon blank when its coefficient is 0, and count the nodes all the way around the ring.

    4. (1 pt) Are the eigenvectors in the output normalized? If not, pick one eigenvector and normalize it.

      Solution — part (d) of question 3

      add the squares of [1,1,1,1][1, 1, 1, 1]: 12+12+12+12=41^{2} + 1^{2} + 1^{2} + 1^{2} = 4 then take the square root: length =4=2= \sqrt{4} = 2

      ↓ length 2: not normalized; divide by 2

      Conclusion

      12[1,1,1,1]=[12,12,12,12]\tfrac{1}{2}\,[1, 1, 1, 1] = \left[\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right]

      Why: or [−1,0,1,0][-1, 0, 1, 0], length 2\sqrt{2}, becomes [−12,0,12,0]\left[-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, 0, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, 0\right]: keep the zeros. Dividing by the sum of the squares (4) gives 14\tfrac{1}{4}’s, which have length 12\tfrac{1}{2}, not 1.

      In words. Find the vector’s length (the square root of the sum of the squares) and divide every entry by it. The result has length 1.

  4. EC. (2 pts) Extra credit. Write the Hamiltonian Ĥ\hat{H} of the hydrogen molecular ion H₂⁺ (two protons, one electron), with derivatives, as in Ĥψ=Eψ\hat{H}\psi = E\psi. Define your symbols for the distances between the particles.

    Solution for question EC

    the electron’s kinetic energy: −ℏ22m∇2-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}

    ↓ plus a Coulomb energy for each pair of charges

    electron–proton attractions (negative): −ke2rA−ke2rB-\frac{ke^{2}}{r_{A}} - \frac{ke^{2}}{r_{B}}

    proton–proton repulsion (positive): +ke2R+\frac{ke^{2}}{R}

    Conclusion

    Ĥ=−ℏ22m∇2−ke2rA−ke2rB+ke2R\hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2} - \frac{ke^{2}}{r_{A}} - \frac{ke^{2}}{r_{B}} + \frac{ke^{2}}{R}

    Why: mm is the electron’s mass and k=1/4πε0k = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_{0}. A Hückel matrix such as (−α−β−β−α)\begin{pmatrix} -\alpha & -\beta \\ -\beta & -\alpha \end{pmatrix} is what Ĥ\hat{H} becomes after choosing atomic orbitals; it has no derivatives.

    In words. The Hamiltonian is the electron’s kinetic energy (the second-derivative term) plus a Coulomb potential energy for each pair of charges: two attractions and one repulsion.

Where the class had the most trouble

These cost the most points. If you lost points here, start your review with them.

  1. Problem 3(c) — the orbitals go on the ring. Only 1 of 13 booklets earned both points. Eight booklets sketched the orbitals on a chain, and nine wrote 3 nodes for [−1,1,−1,1][-1, 1, -1, 1]. On the ring C4 is bonded back to C1, so that orbital changes sign four times: 2 nodal lines (4 nodal points). Five booklets drew an orbital at a carbon whose coefficient is 0; a zero means nothing is drawn there.
  2. Problem 1(b) — the eigenvectors of a diagonal matrix. Six booklets did not write the vectors with a single 1: two wrote the eigenvalues themselves (6, 7, 2, 4) as the eigenvectors, two wrote vectors that are not eigenvectors of this matrix, one divided the right vectors by 105\sqrt{105}, and one left it blank. Each eigenvector is a single 1 in its eigenvalue’s row, and it already has length 1.
  3. Problem 3(d) — normalizing. Two booklets answered that the vectors are already normalized, and one divided by the sum of the squares. The length is the square root of the sum of the squares: [1,1,1,1][1, 1, 1, 1] has length 2, so it becomes [12,12,12,12]\left[\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right]. Keep the zero entries.
  4. Problem 3(a) — chain or ring. Three booklets drew a chain, and two wrote 2 or 3 for the number of unsaturated carbons. The −β-\beta in the corners of HH bonds C4 to C1: a ring of four carbons, one H on each.
  5. Problem 3(b) — two levels at −1 eV. Three booklets drew a single −1 eV line, and two put the pair at −2 eV. “Multiplicity 2” means two separate levels at −1 eV.
  6. Extra credit — the Hamiltonian with derivatives. Nobody earned extra credit. Most answers were Hückel matrices; the question asked for the operator: the kinetic-energy term plus a Coulomb term for each pair of charges.
