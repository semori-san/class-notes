Version A — multiple choice

Booklets with odd numbers (stamp A-0XX). Exactly as administered on Friday, September 11, 2026: 5 questions worth 1 point each.

  1. A digital camera sensor has an area of 1 cm2^{2} and contains 10810^{8} identical square pixels. What is the width of one pixel?

    1. 10−1210^{-12} m2^{2}
    2. 10−610^{-6} m
    3. 10−610^{-6} m2^{2}
    4. 10−410^{-4} m
    5. 10−210^{-2} m
    Solution for question 1

    Correct answer: B. 10−610^{-6} m

    Why: Convert the area first: 1cm2=(10−2m)2=10−4m21\,\text{cm}^{2}=(10^{-2}\,\text{m})^{2}=10^{-4}\,\text{m}^{2}. Share it among the pixels: 10−4÷108=10−12m210^{-4}\div10^{8}=10^{-12}\,\text{m}^{2} per pixel. A width is the square root of a square’s area: 10−12=10−6\sqrt{10^{-12}}=10^{-6} m, about a micrometer. 10−1210^{-12} m2^{2} is the area of one pixel, with no square root taken. 10−610^{-6} m2^{2} has the right number with an area unit on a width. 10−410^{-4} m is the sensor’s own area written as if it were a length, and 10−210^{-2} m is the sensor’s side, never divided among the pixels.

  2. On a base-10 logarithmic axis, the distance from 10 to 100 is the same as the distance from 100 to 1000. Which statement best explains why?

    1. Equal distances represent equal differences, and the two intervals span the same amount.
    2. Abraham Lincoln was born in a log cabin built from exactly 100 logs, and 100 appears in both intervals.
    3. Large numbers are compressed, so every interval on the axis eventually has the same length.
    4. Each of the two intervals contains the same count of whole numbers.
    5. Equal distances represent equal multiplying factors, and each interval is one factor of 10.
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: E. Equal distances represent equal multiplying factors, and each interval is one factor of 10.

    Why: One step along a base-10 log axis means multiply by 10, not add a fixed amount: 10 to 100 is ×10\times10, and so is 100 to 1000. That is the whole point of a log scale (HW Problem 1: a linear step is x→x+cx\to x+c, a log step is x→x×cx\to x\times c). The “equal differences” choice is the linear-axis intuition, and it is false here anyway, since 100−10≠1000−100100-10\neq1000-100. The two intervals do not hold the same count of whole numbers either (91 versus 901). “Large numbers are compressed” describes how a log axis looks, but it is not the rule, and it is false as stated: the stretch from 1000 to 1001 is nowhere near as long as the one from 100 to 1000. The Lincoln choice is only a pun on “log.”

    This question also appears on Version B (as question 4) with its choices in reverse order.

  3. A square solar panel is tiled with 10610^{6} identical square cells. Each cell has an area of 10−410^{-4} m2^{2}. What is the width of the panel?

    1. 10−210^{-2} m
    2. 1010 m
    3. 1010 m2^{2}
    4. 10210^{2} m
    5. 10210^{2} m2^{2}
    Solution for question 3

    Correct answer: B. 1010 m

    Why: Total area first: 106×10−4m2=102m210^{6}\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}^{2}=10^{2}\,\text{m}^{2}. Then the width of that square: 102=10\sqrt{10^{2}}=10 m. Choosing 10210^{2} m means finding the area correctly and then writing that number down as a width. This was the most common slip on the width questions: a width is always the square root of the area. 10210^{2} m2^{2} is the panel’s area, 1010 m2^{2} puts an area unit on a width, and 10−210^{-2} m is one cell’s width, never scaled up to the whole panel.

  4. If log(1)=0\log(1)=0, log(10)=1\log(10)=1, log(100)=2\log(100)=2, and log(1000)=3\log(1000)=3, then log(30)\log(30) is approximately:

    1. 0.50.5
    2. 1.21.2
    3. 1.51.5
    4. 1.81.8
    5. 2.52.5
    Solution for question 4

    Correct answer: C. 1.51.5

    Why: Split 30 into a factor of 10 and a factor of 3: log(30)=log(3)+log(10)≈0.5+1=1.5\log(30)=\log(3)+\log(10)\approx0.5+1=1.5. Why is log(3)≈0.5\log(3)\approx0.5? Halfway across a decade on a log axis is 10≈3.16\sqrt{10}\approx3.16 (HW Problem 2(c)), so 3 sits almost exactly halfway between 1 and 10. The most common wrong answer on the quiz was 1.2. It comes from placing 30 about 20/90≈0.2220/90\approx0.22 of the way from 10 to 100, as if the axis were linear. On a log axis equal distances are equal ratios, which is exactly what the other log question on your quiz asks about. 1.8 puts 3 near the top of its decade instead of the middle; 2.5 is log(300)\log(300), one decade too many; and 0.5 is log(3)\log(3) with the factor of 10 left off.

    This question also appears on Version B (as question 2) with its choices in reverse order.

  5. A cell-culture plate has a growing region measuring 1 cm ×\times 10 cm. Roughly how many bacteria (1 μ\mum across, modeled as squares) would cover it in a single layer?

    1. 101110^{11} m2^{2}
    2. 101110^{11}
    3. 10910^{9} m2^{2}
    4. 10910^{9}
    5. 10310^{3}
    Solution for question 5

    Correct answer: D. 10910^{9}

    Why: Convert each side to meters before multiplying: 10−2m×10−1m=10−3m210^{-2}\,\text{m}\times10^{-1}\,\text{m}=10^{-3}\,\text{m}^{2}. One bacterium covers (10−6m)2=10−12m2(10^{-6}\,\text{m})^{2}=10^{-12}\,\text{m}^{2}. Area ÷ area: 10−3÷10−12=10910^{-3}\div10^{-12}=10^{9}, a pure count with no units. 101110^{11} comes from turning 10 cm2^{2} into 10−110^{-1} m2^{2}, converting an area as if it were a length (1 cm2^{2} is 10−410^{-4} m2^{2}, not 10−210^{-2}). 10310^{3} treats the bacterium’s width as its area. The m2^{2} choices put an area unit on a count.

Part II — free response (both versions)

5 points, plus 1 extra-credit point for part (c).

In the far future, the “ultimate transistor” might be only ten (10) atoms wide.

(a) (1.5 pt) What would be the approximate width of this “ultimate transistor” in the unit of nm? Clearly indicate your assumption for the width of each atom.

Solution for part (a)

Assume one atom is about 0.10.1 nm across (that is 11 Å, or 10−1010^{-10} m). Ten atoms side by side: 10×0.1nm=1nm10\times0.1\,\text{nm}=1\,\text{nm}. Any atom width from about 0.05 to 0.5 nm is reasonable, so a width from 0.5 to 5 nm is a good answer.

Watch out: converting 10−1010^{-10} m to 0.10.1 nm gives the width of one atom. The transistor is ten of them, so you still have to multiply by 10.

How it was graded: 0.5 for stating an atom width with a unit; 0.5 for multiplying it by ten; 0.5 for a final width with a length unit that works out to 0.5–5 nm. Any length unit is fine: 1 nm, 10 Å and 10−910^{-9} m all earn it.

(b) (3.5 pt) Based on your answer above, about how many “ultimate transistors” would fit in the area of a 10 mm × 10 mm chip? Show your calculations to perform the estimate. As per usual, you may treat each transistor as a square.

Solution for part (b)

Route 1: area ÷ area (in meters). Chip edge: 10mm=10×10−3m=10−2m10\,\text{mm}=10\times10^{-3}\,\text{m}=10^{-2}\,\text{m}, so the chip area is (10−2m)2=10−4m2(10^{-2}\,\text{m})^{2}=10^{-4}\,\text{m}^{2}. One transistor: (10−9m)2=10−18m2(10^{-9}\,\text{m})^{2}=10^{-18}\,\text{m}^{2}. Count: 10−4÷10−18=101410^{-4}\div10^{-18}=10^{14} transistors.

Route 2: count along one edge, then square. 10mm=107nm10\,\text{mm}=10^{7}\,\text{nm}, so 10710^{7} transistors fit along one edge, and (107)2=1014(10^{7})^{2}=10^{14} fit on the chip.

Does it make sense? A modern chip holds roughly 101010^{10} transistors, so 101410^{14} ultimate transistors is about 10410^{4} times more. That is plausible for transistors about 100 times narrower. An answer below 1, or one carrying m2^{2}, is a sign the division ran the wrong way or the units did not cancel.

How it was graded: 0.75 for getting the 10 mm chip size into the right units; 0.5 for using your own part-(a) transistor size in the division; 1.5 for accounting for both dimensions (squaring); 0.75 for a final answer that is a plain count, greater than one, with no units. A mistake was charged once, where it happened. Later steps were graded against your numbers, so a wrong width in (a) did not cost you again in (b).

(c) (1 extra-credit pt) Extra credit. About how many orders of magnitude smaller is the area of one “ultimate transistor” than the area of a transistor built today? Clearly state the width you assume for a transistor built today.

Solution for part (c)

Assume today’s transistor is about 100100 nm wide. Its area is (100nm)2=104nm2(100\,\text{nm})^{2}=10^{4}\,\text{nm}^{2}; the ultimate transistor’s is (1nm)2=1nm2(1\,\text{nm})^{2}=1\,\text{nm}^{2}. Ratio: 10410^{4}, so about 4 orders of magnitude smaller in area.

Why not 2? The widths differ by 10210^{2}, but area goes as width squared, so the areas differ by (102)2=104(10^{2})^{2}=10^{4}. Comparing widths instead of areas halves the exponent.

How it was graded: 0.25 for stating today’s transistor width with a unit (anything from 1 nm to 10 µm was accepted); 0.75 for comparing areas and getting an order-of-magnitude count that follows from your own two widths. Comparing widths only earned 0.25 of that. Extra credit can only add to your score.

What tripped people up

The slips that cost the most points across the class, most common first. If one of these is yours, it is worth redoing that question from scratch.

  • Converting 10 mm to meters. 10mm=10×10−3m=10−210\,\text{mm}=10\times10^{-3}\,\text{m}=10^{-2} m, not 10−310^{-3} m. Losing that factor of 10 (or squaring it into a factor of 100) was the most common slip in part (b).
  • Estimating a log that falls between powers of ten. log(30)\log(30) is not 1.2. Split it into log(3)+log(10)\log(3)+\log(10), and remember that 3 is roughly halfway across a decade.
  • Getting a width from an area. Once you have the total area of a square, take the square root. Writing the area’s number down with a length unit was the usual wrong answer on the width questions.
  • The “ten atoms” step. 10−1010^{-10} m =0.1=0.1 nm is one atom. The transistor is ten atoms, so multiply by 10.
  • Checking the answer. A count of 10−410^{-4} transistors, or a count in m2^{2}, cannot be right. A ten-second sanity check catches an upside-down division.
  • Areas in part (c). The question compares areas. Squaring both widths turns “2 orders of magnitude” into the correct “4.”
