Version A — multiple choice

Booklets with odd numbers (stamp A-0XX). Exactly as administered on Friday, September 18, 2026: 5 questions worth 1 point each.

  1. Fill in the blanks: It is difficult to pattern or image objects that are smaller than the blank of the wave (like light), because of the blank limit.

    1. wavelength … quantum
    2. wavelength … diffraction
    3. amplitude … diffraction
    4. frequency … photoresist
    5. brightness … exposure
    Solution for question 1

    Correct answer: B. wavelength … diffraction

    Why: Light spreads out (diffracts) when it passes through an opening comparable to or smaller than its wavelength, so features smaller than about one wavelength blur together. That is the diffraction limit (lecture 9/11; HW 9/14 Problem 3). “Wavelength … quantum” has the right first blank but the wrong name for the limit. “Amplitude … diffraction” names the right limit but the wrong property: amplitude is how strong the wave is, not a length. Frequency is not a length either, so nothing can be “smaller than” it, and there is no “photoresist limit.” Brighter light does not print smaller features, and there is no “exposure limit” in this sense.

  2. If log⁡(1)=0\log(1)=0, log⁡(10)=1\log(10)=1, log⁡(100)=2\log(100)=2, and log⁡(1000)=3\log(1000)=3, then log⁡(300)\log(300) is approximately:

    1. 0.50.5
    2. 1.51.5
    3. 2.22.2
    4. 2.52.5
    5. 3.53.5
    Solution for question 2

    Correct answer: D. 2.52.5

    Why: Split 300 into 3×1003\times100: log⁡(300)=log⁡(3)+log⁡(100)≈0.5+2=2.5\log(300)=\log(3)+\log(100)\approx0.5+2=2.5. On a log scale 3 sits roughly halfway across a decade, so log⁡(3)≈0.5\log(3)\approx0.5. 2.2 is the straight-line trap: 300 is about 22% of the way from 100 to 1000, but a log axis is not linear. 0.5 is log⁡(3)\log(3) without the two decades from the ×100\times100, 1.5 is log⁡(30)\log(30), and 3.5 is log⁡(3000)\log(3000).

  3. Photolithography includes these four processes: expose, develop, mask align, and spin coat. In what order are they performed?

    1. spin coat → mask align → expose → develop
    2. spin coat → expose → mask align → develop
    3. mask align → spin coat → expose → develop
    4. spin coat → mask align → develop → expose
    5. spin coat → develop → mask align → expose
    Solution for question 3

    Correct answer: A. spin coat → mask align → expose → develop

    Why: Spin-coat the wafer with photoresist, align the mask over it, expose the resist to light through the mask, then develop it to reveal the pattern (lecture 9/11). The mask cannot be aligned before there is resist on the wafer to pattern. Exposing before the mask is in place exposes the whole resist layer, with no pattern. And developing before exposing does nothing: development only works on a pattern that exposure has already written into the resist.

  4. A square LED display is tiled with 10610^{6} identical square pixels. Each pixel has an area of 10−810^{-8} m2^{2}. What is the width of the display?

    1. 10−410^{-4} m
    2. 10−210^{-2} m2^{2}
    3. 10−210^{-2} m
    4. 10−110^{-1} m2^{2}
    5. 10−110^{-1} m
    Solution for question 4

    Correct answer: E. 10−110^{-1} m

    Why: Find the display’s area first: 106×10−8m2=10−2m210^{6}\times10^{-8}\,\text{m}^{2}=10^{-2}\,\text{m}^{2}. A width is the square root of a square’s area: 10−2m2=10−1\sqrt{10^{-2}\,\text{m}^{2}}=10^{-1} m, or 10 cm. 10−210^{-2} m is that area’s number written as a length, with no square root taken. 10−210^{-2} m2^{2} is the area itself, not a width, and 10−110^{-1} m2^{2} has the right number with an area unit on a width. 10−410^{-4} m is the width of a single pixel (10−8m2\sqrt{10^{-8}\,\text{m}^{2}}), never scaled up to the whole display.

  5. The observation that the number of transistors on a computer chip approximately doubles about every two years is known as blank’s Law.

    1. Ohm
    2. Murphy
    3. Moore
    4. Planck
    5. Metcalfe
    Solution for question 5

    Correct answer: C. Moore

    Why: Moore’s Law is named after Intel co-founder Gordon Moore, who observed that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years (HW 9/14 Problem 1). Ohm’s Law, V=IRV=IR, relates voltage, current and resistance. Murphy’s Law (“anything that can go wrong will go wrong”) is a joke, not a trend in chips. Planck’s law describes the light given off by hot objects, and Metcalfe’s Law says a network’s value grows with the square of its number of users.

Part II — free response (both versions)

5 points: (a) 2.5, (b) 1.5 and (c) 1. There was no extra credit on this quiz.

A mysterious businessman, Mr. Urotas Irome, claims that his electronics company sells a mini-chip that stores 100 trillion transistors in an area of about 1 mm2^{2} (1 millimeter squared).

(a) (2.5 pt) What would be the area of each transistor? For full credit, please include appropriate units in your calculations and answer.

Solution for part (a)

Count: 100 trillion =100×1012=1014=100\times10^{12}=10^{14} transistors. (One trillion is 101210^{12}.)

Chip area in square meters: 1mm=10−3m1\,\text{mm}=10^{-3}\,\text{m}, so 1mm2=(10−3m)2=10−6m21\,\text{mm}^{2}=(10^{-3}\,\text{m})^{2}=10^{-6}\,\text{m}^{2}.

Share it among the transistors: 10−6m2÷1014=10−6−14m2=10−20m210^{-6}\,\text{m}^{2}\div10^{14}=10^{-6-14}\,\text{m}^{2}=10^{-20}\,\text{m}^{2} per transistor. The same area is 10−14mm210^{-14}\,\text{mm}^{2} or 0.01nm20.01\,\text{nm}^{2}; any area unit is fine.

Watch out: an area conversion squares the length factor. 1mm21\,\text{mm}^{2} is 10−6m210^{-6}\,\text{m}^{2}, not 10−3m210^{-3}\,\text{m}^{2}. And count your zeros: 101210^{12} is one trillion, so 100 trillion is two powers of ten more.

How it was graded: 0.75 for turning 100 trillion into 101410^{14} (graded on the number you actually divided by); 0.75 for converting 1 mm2^{2} with the conversion factor squared; 1.0 for dividing the area by the count, in that order and with the exponents right, with an area unit on the answer. An answer with no units at all lost 0.25 in each of the last two. A mistake was charged once, where it happened: if your count was off, your division was graded against your count.

(b) (1.5 pt) Assuming that each transistor is a square, what is its width? For full credit, please include appropriate units in your calculations and answer.

Solution for part (b)

A square’s area is its width squared, so its width is the square root of its area: 10−20m2=10−10m=0.1nm\sqrt{10^{-20}\,\text{m}^{2}}=10^{-10}\,\text{m}=0.1\,\text{nm}. Taking the square root halves the exponent.

If your area had an odd exponent, the root is fine written as 10−8.510^{-8.5} m or about 3×10−93\times10^{-9} m. A √ sign, a ½ power, or solving A=w2A=w^{2} for ww all count.

How it was graded: 1.0 for taking the square root of your own part-(a) area; 0.5 for giving the width a length unit such as m or nm, not m2^{2}. A wrong area in (a) did not cost you again here.

(c) (1 pt) Do you think Mr. Irome’s claim is reasonable and credible? Why or why not?

Solution for part (c)

Not credible. A width of 0.1 nm is about the size of a single atom (0.1–0.3 nm), and a working transistor needs many atoms. Quiz 3’s “ultimate transistor” was already ten atoms wide.

It is also about 100 times smaller than the 13.5 nm wavelength of the EUV light used to pattern today’s chips, far beyond the diffraction limit. For scale, a modern chip holds about 101010^{10} transistors on about 1 cm2^{2}, roughly 10810^{8} per mm2^{2}: a million times fewer than claimed.

How it was graded: 0.5 for comparing your size with something definite: an atom, the wavelength of light or the diffraction limit, today’s transistors, Moore’s Law, or even the chip itself. Saying only “too small” or “we can’t make that yet” earned 0.25. 0.5 for a clear conclusion that follows from your own comparison (“probably not” counts). Either verdict could earn full credit if your own numbers supported it.

What tripped people up

The slips that cost the most points across the class, most common first. If one of these is yours, it is worth redoing that question from scratch.

  • Turning “100 trillion” into a power of ten. One trillion is 101210^{12}, so 100 trillion is 100×1012=1014100\times10^{12}=10^{14}. This was the most common slip on the quiz. If you write the number out, count the zeros twice.
  • Exponents in a division, and which way it goes. 10−6÷1014=10−6−14=10−2010^{-6}\div10^{14}=10^{-6-14}=10^{-20}: dividing subtracts exponents. Divide the area by the count, not the other way round. A “per-transistor” area bigger than the whole chip is a sign the division ran backwards.
  • Backing a verdict with a yardstick. “That’s too small” is a start. “That’s about the width of one atom” or “that’s 100 times smaller than the wavelength of EUV light” is an argument.
  • Getting a width from an area. Once you have the total area of a square, take the square root. On both multiple-choice width questions, every wrong answer was the total area, with no square root taken.
  • The photolithography order. Spin coat → mask align → expose → develop. You can only develop a pattern that exposure has already written into the resist.
  • Estimating a log between powers of ten. log⁡(300)=log⁡(3)+log⁡(100)≈0.5+2\log(300)=\log(3)+\log(100)\approx0.5+2. Much better than on Quiz 3, but the straight-line answer (2.2, or 3.2 for 3000) still caught a few people.
