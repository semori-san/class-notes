Peering into the Nanoscale: Characterization and Imaging

When we synthesize nanoparticles in the lab, we face an immediate problem: they are far too small to see with our own eyes. So, how do we know what we have actually made?

To solve this, we rely on advanced characterization technologies. Two of the most classic tools are the Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) and the Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM). Think of the SEM as a way to look at the surface morphology—it shows us the 3D shape and texture of the outside of our particles. The TEM, on the other hand, allows us to look through the particle, revealing its inner structure. Are the particles solid? Are they porous? Are they hollow? The TEM gives us these answers.

If you wanted to check if your newly synthesized nanoparticles have a rough, bumpy exterior, which microscope would you use, and why?

You would use a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM). The SEM is specifically designed to show the 3D surface morphology and texture of the outside of the particles, whereas the TEM is used to look through the particle to see its internal structure.

But seeing the particle in a dish is only step one. How do we know if our nanoparticles actually work as a medicine?

To evaluate anti-cancer therapies, we use mouse models. We carefully introduce cancer cells into the skin of the mouse to grow a tumor. Once the tumor reaches a suitable size, we administer our nanoparticle drugs. Over a period of days (as seen in the 14-day timeline above), we monitor the tumor to see if our drug is successfully shrinking it.

But wait—once we inject the drug, how do we know where it goes? Does it actually reach the tumor, or does it get lost in the liver or kidneys? To answer this, we design our nanoparticles to act as bioimaging agents. By incorporating specific chemical dyes (like phthalocyanines) into our nanoparticles, we can shine a laser on the mouse, and the drug will emit a fluorescent signal. Alternatively, some molecules produce heat when exposed to a laser, allowing us to use infrared (IR) thermal cameras to track the drug’s location. This allows us to map the biodistribution of the drug in real-time, completely non-invasively!

Smart Delivery: Designing Nanoparticle Vehicles

Now that we know how to see and track our particles, how do we actually design them to fight disease? Let’s look at a few fascinating strategies.

Liposomal Nanoparticles: The pH-Sensitive Delivery Truck

Imagine you want to deliver a highly toxic payload to a cancer cell without harming the healthy cells along the way. How do you build a secure delivery truck? One excellent method is using a liposome—a tiny, spherical vesicle made of lipid molecules that can encapsulate drugs.

In the design above, we pack the liposome with two key ingredients: Artesunate (ARS) and Indocyanine Green (ICG). Why these two?

  • ARS is used to generate Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS). Think of ROS as chemical wrecking balls—they cause immense oxidative stress to cancer cells, destroying them from the inside out.

  • ICG acts as both an imaging agent (so we can track it) and an in-situ heat source.

We engineer the lipid shell to be pH-sensitive. Tumors naturally have a slightly more acidic environment than healthy tissue. When the liposome reaches the acidic tumor, the shell destabilizes. We then shine an 808 nm laser onto the tumor site. The ICG absorbs the light and generates intense heat (Photothermal Therapy, or PTT), while the ARS releases the destructive ROS (Thermodynamic Therapy, or TDT). It is a highly targeted, one-two punch against the cancer!

Self-Assembled Nanoparticles: Reaching Deep Tissues

Light is a fantastic trigger, but it has a limitation: it cannot penetrate very deeply into the human body. What if the tumor is located deep inside an organ?

Instead of light, we can use ultrasound, which penetrates deep into tissues effortlessly. This approach is called Sonodynamic Therapy (SDT). In this design, we don’t even need to build a lipid shell. We use molecules (like Artesunate-phthalocyanine) that naturally self-assemble into nanostructured aggregates when placed in water.

When these aggregates accumulate in the tumor and are hit with ultrasound waves, they cause a phenomenon called cavitation—the rapid formation and collapse of tiny bubbles. This violent mechanical action, combined with the chemical release of ROS, destroys the deep-tissue tumor with remarkable efficiency.

Why might a doctor choose Sonodynamic Therapy (SDT) over Photothermal Therapy (PTT) for a tumor located deep inside the liver?

Light from a laser cannot penetrate very deeply into the human body, making light-triggered therapies like PTT ineffective for deep organs. Ultrasound waves, however, can penetrate deep tissues effortlessly to trigger the nanoparticles and destroy the tumor.

Wearable Nanotech: The End of the Syringe?

We have talked a lot about injecting these nanoparticles. But let’s be honest—nobody likes syringes. They are painful, they require a trained professional to administer, and they can be intimidating. How can we make nanomedicine more accessible and painless?

Enter the microneedle patch.

A microneedle patch is a tiny square, smaller than a penny, covered in microscopic needles that penetrate just the very top, painless layer of the skin. It is incredibly convenient—you just press it on like a bandage.

Let’s look at a brilliant application of this platform developed right here by Virginia Tech researchers: combating the opioid crisis. The researchers used mesoporous silica nanoparticles—think of them as tiny nanoscale sponges with thousands of microscopic pores. They filled these pores with naloxone, a drug that reverses fentanyl overdoses.

But how does the patch know when to release the drug? They capped the pores with a special molecular "gate" (an aptamer) that remains locked shut under normal conditions. However, if the patient experiences an overdose, the presence of fentanyl in the body acts as a chemical key. The fentanyl unlocks the gate, and the nanoparticle instantly releases the naloxone, reversing the lethal dose automatically. This is the true power of smart nanomedicine!

Classroom Demonstration: The Tyndall Effect

All of these complex platforms start with a very basic step: successfully fabricating a nanoparticle solution. I brought two vials to class today. One contains pure water, and the other contains a liposomal nanoparticle solution I synthesized.

If you look closely at them, can you see a difference? No, right? To the naked eye, they both look like perfectly clear, transparent water.

So, if we don’t have a multi-million-dollar electron microscope in this classroom, how can we prove that one of these vials is full of nanoparticles? We can use a simple physical phenomenon called the Tyndall effect.

When I shine a standard laser pointer through the vial of pure water, the light passes straight through invisibly. But watch what happens when I shine the laser through the nanoparticle solution: a bright, solid line of light appears suspended in the liquid!

Why does this happen? The nanoparticles in the solution are incredibly small, but they are just large enough to scatter the laser light. As the light hits the particles, it bounces off them and into our eyes, illuminating the path of the beam. This simple scattering test is a fundamental, rapid way to verify the presence of nanoparticles in a colloidal suspension.

You shine a laser pointer through a clear liquid, but the beam remains completely invisible as it passes through the vial. What does this tell you about the liquid?

It tells you that the liquid is likely pure and does not contain nanoparticles. If nanoparticles were present, they would be just large enough to scatter the laser light into your eyes, making the beam’s path visible via the Tyndall effect.
